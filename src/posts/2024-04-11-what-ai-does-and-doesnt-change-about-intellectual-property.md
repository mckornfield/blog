### I Fought the Law and the Law Slowly Caught Up

I recently heard a podcast episode where one of the speakers referenced this [Edward O. Wilson quote](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/9770741-the-real-problem-of-humanity-is-the-following-we-have): “…We have Paleolithic emotions, medieval institutions and godlike technology…”

Intellectual property law is old, but [laws are being thrown around](https://thehill.com/homenews/house/4583318-schiff-unveils-ai-training-transparency-measure/) to try and stem the tide of things these new AI tools are capable of doing, which are able to learn pretty darn fast to write text and produce images ([even with weird hands](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=24yjRbBah3w)).

But violating copyright and other forms of IP law are nothing new; you can [apparently steal Marvin Gaye’s “vibe”](https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/music/robin-thicke-pharrell-williams-pay-5-million-marvin-gaye-estate-n947666), all without the power of AI, or even more ridiculous is how [YouTube enforces copyright via copyright strikes](https://medium.com/@bryanvannorden_14478/youtube-copyright-is-broken-383fb732a421).

My goal is not to debate the merits of copyright, but rather, see what sort of spice Machine Learning will add to an already messy dish. Let’s start with the basics —

### “I Know Kung Fu”

In *The Matrix,* Neo needs to be able to learn how to fight. [A (now dated) computed animation pops up on the screen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6vMO3XmNXe4) as his brain is pumped full of all sorts of different fighting styles. The line delivery he gives after this cramming session is legendary — “I know kung fu.”

These machine learning models, wheth[er trained on an internet’s worth of content](https://community.openai.com/t/what-is-the-size-of-the-training-set-for-gpt-3/360896), or a [handful of pictures of something in slightly different poses](https://tryolabs.com/blog/2022/10/25/the-guide-to-fine-tuning-stable-diffusion-with-your-own-images), learn slower than Neo does in this clip, but MUCH faster than you’d consider a human or any other animal would learn.

My daughter, as an example, can draw a few sets of lines going the same direction (she’s two) and speak a handful of words, but she is no ChatGPT or DALL-E. She’s going to take years to get to even drawing stick figures and scrawling out her name in barely readable kindergarten hand writing.

Many many hours of GPUs churning through datasets in parallel gives a training time on the order of hours or days, not the years that humans take to learn this information. And then within a very short window (seconds, minutes), these tools can “infer” from prompts to generate text and images. Even the fastest typist or street artist couldn’t match these models in action.

Fundamentally these two types of learning are different (etched in silicon not synapses), but the inputs and outputs can be pretty well tied together (representations of the world, in written or pictorial form). The source of the knowledge that a kid learns from and the source that these machines are trained on aren’t too far off; the difference is *speed,* of learning AND of production.

Computers can learn kung fu faster than we can. And why is that? Well —

### Computers Can Do Stupid Tasks Really Fast

If learning is the act of building neural connections, pruning unused ones, and then sheathing/strengthening the connections, then we have a pretty narrow definition of learning.

Machine Learning/ Artificial Intelligence in its current form can be likened to this type of learning, but it’s actually closer to a giant army of people doing “basic” math problems (although “basic” might be disingenuous).

The GPUs that are powering this revolution are “graphics processing units”, built for doing lots of math calculations extremely fast to power displays of video games.

But the reapplication of these amazing math skills to solve the problem of “what is the next piece of text after this prompt” or “an image that matches this text prompt” is —

### When the Stupid Tasks Become Magical Tasks

Once you have enough well labelled data, and a decent enough training algorithm, these amazing math skills can produce a model that, in practically real time, produces text and images worthy of the term “magic.”

The fact that a machine, who has never seen a picture of Captain America, can draw me in a knockoff version of him within a few minutes is “magical”.

*Captain America me in space? I guess?*

The power of these tools to do things like produce [fake videos](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3vHvOyZ0GbY), copies of music, of text, is a function of their ability to learn kung-fu and also perform kung-fu *fast*.

We can easily criticize these tools (hallucinations, hand drawing, etc.) but to give a person with an internet connection the ability to take their drawing or writing level from “bad” to “pretty decent” really fits with the “god like technology” quote from above.

How do these god-like tools change the IP landscape? Well we know that —

### Fakers Gonna Fake, Stealers Gonna Steal

As long as there’s a way to make money off of imitating or stealing an idea, the idea will be imitated/stolen. People will sell knock [off Disney gear](https://www.clickorlando.com/theme-parks/2022/12/30/disney-sues-kissimmee-online-sellers-over-knockoff-mickey-ears-other-goods-lawsuit-claims/), and commit all sorts of other fraud/intellectual property violations if there’s a dollar to be made.

But if you want to make [a fake Taylor Swift song](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZYATta5yGtQ), or impersonate [Keanu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3dBiNGufIJw) (remember, he knows kung fu), the tools to produce these fakes are better than they’ve ever been.

The legal system is not going to be prepared, not because these fakes have never been done before, but because they’re going to be omnipresent and of relatively high quality for small amounts of effort.

It used to be if you wanted to produce a fake video, you’d have to photoshop frame by frame the actor in, something that no sane person would ever do. But hand an ML model enough training data, and a video of a similar enough looking person, they can do the matching for you in a relatively short period of time.

The faking will get better, it will become more accessible, and (in my opinion), the legal system will not be sufficient to handle this level of theft.

So what are we to do?

### ML Systems Do Not Have Value Systems, But We Do

These ML tools don’t know that they’re committing fraud or stealing, so we either need to stop them in their tracks

There’s only two ways I can think to stop this problem of IP theft:

1. Stop the tools themselves from producing the content (built in copyright enforcement)
2. Using other models to identify fradulent content

You can already see (1) with ChatGPT (I’m sure the record companies sued):

![](/img/what-ai-does-and-doesnt-change-about-intellectual-property/1_gjwwnJZuIuslLSAF3p03rA.png.png)

(2) I think is where there’s more room for improvement. There are tools like [Copyleaks](https://copyleaks.com/book-a-demo) for text, but I don’t know of similar tools for images/videos. And as the tools improve, the difficulty of detecting this content will increase.

I’ll be a bit speculative here: simply trying to detect fake content might become a relatively impossible task, so what might be the gold standard is creating a “thumbprint” that models leave when they produce content, sort of like the serial number on a firearm.

This could be a small watermark, or an artifact in the file if it’s an image, or some sort of “signature” if it’s a piece of written text.

Conversely, you could produce something to show that your content is “human generated”, i.e. a recording of you typing it out (maybe too Big Brother?), or a “human mark.”

So to stop these supercharged IP theft tools from committing fraud, we either have to neuter them or leave something where a blacklight will show us what they really are.

### Summary

AI tools don’t necessarily create new mechanisms to steal intellectual property, but can make much more convincing copies, much more quickly and with a volume that will overwhelm our medieval justice system. Better, faster, stronger, but not necessarily *novel*. (At least not yet).
