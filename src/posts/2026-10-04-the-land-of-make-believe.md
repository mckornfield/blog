I have lived in California for a year now. I have decided I live in a place that doesn’t exist except in people’s minds. I just want to speak about what I’ve seen and experienced. I live in San Diego, so I get a great mix of SoCal’s beautiful people with *la frontera.*

### Homelessness

California has a homeless *situation.* I would say “problem” but that implies California is attempting to solve it.

California is not attempting to solve it.

If anything, it’s attempting to **grow** it.

I also refuse to use the term unhoused. Sue me.

I took the public bus and a homeless man sat next to me, rubbing his lottery tickets together like the rosary, all while wearing his KN-95 mask ([the COVID pandemic is over, don’t ya know)](https://www.newsweek.com/lesbian-bar-mask-mandate-last-ditch-massachusetts-backlash-12480852).

As we drove down University Avenue, I saw a panoply of homeless of different shapes, sizes, abilities, all marked by how downtrodden and dirty they appeared. And almost outnumbering the number of “housed” people around them.

A few weeks ago I talked to a guy at the bus stop who had problems with his wife, ended up on the street, and was consuming alcohol out of an electrolye bottle (the irony was not lost on me). This is at like 8:00 AM.

He said he drinks a 5th a day. That… doesn’t seem like it’s helping?

*Patient: Doc, when I do this, it hurts!*

*Doc: Well stop doing that!*

As a kid I had a lot of empathy for homeless people, but I can’t help but feel a certain amount of… “what the hell are we doing here?” How is this an improvement on… anything?

In my neighborhood, next to our apartment block, there are (arguably very overpiced) Million+ dollar homes… and there’s a dude sleeping on a sheet of cardboard right next to it.

There is a cute park where my daughter plays, next to her elementary school, and *twice* I have seen an ambulance drag a homeless person away who probably had overdosed.

There are just under 200,000 homeless people in CA. 0.5% of the population. 1 in every 200 people is homeless here.

[And most of them are unsheltered](https://mckornfield.github.io/homelessness-stats/).

You can’t build anything here, and you can’t rent anything even sort of decent without a decent size paycheck. And let us not forget: there are lots and lots of pro homeless policies and activities that the state and federal government fund.

California can’t enforce its laws against encamping because *these people have no place to go*. There aren’t enough shelters and the other states

San Diego spent [2 billion dollars to only add 200 beds](https://www.rtfhsd.org/san-diego-city-budget-adds-shelter-beds-reduces-some-services/). There are 5000 unsheltered homeless people in the city. At “2700 shelter options”, the government is not moving at any decent rate to even try to house them.

But we can pretend they are. We can pretend that the city and the state are doing their very bestest to help the homeless.

They’re at least spending money like they are.

But as far as actual results: it’s make believe.

### Climate Change

You can read [*Abundance*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_%28Klein_and_Thompson_book%29) for the real takedown of California’s inability to build any high speed rail or significant climate infrastructure. But I want to talk about what I’ve seen and experienced.

California acts like it cares about the climate, but what it really cares about are *its* *incumbents.* If you’ve been living here for years and years, you have a nice amount of equity in your house, and some nice solar panels on your roof, i.e., you’re making a difference for the planet.

But if you just showed up here, renting is your only way unless you make *very* good money. And no, you won’t have solar on your rental, you’ll have the most expensive electricity in the [USA other than Hawaii](https://www.electricchoice.com/electricity-prices-by-state/) (let’s remember, Hawaii is… an island?)

The only thing that is sort of cheap in this state (sort of, it’s still more expensive than anywhere else I’ve lived) is gas (*laughs in Farsi*). People heat their homes with gas down in San Diego because it’s *much* cheaper than the (bonafidely corrupt) utility here that overcharges for electricity.

Electricty is “cheap” if you have solar panels and own your house, but as we established, it’s hard to break into home ownership (see zoning and Prop 13). So what we’re left with is rich/old people with Teslas and solar panels, and an army of renters driving gas cars of all shapes and sizes, getting their paycheck sieved away by sh\*ty utility companies and the [highest gas prices in the country](https://gasprices.aaa.com/state-gas-price-averages/) (I know… it’s just all expensive).

Here’s a [graph](https://www.reddit.com/r/charts/comments/1t5ud20/texas_vs_california_in_utility_scale_solar_energy/) supporting my whole shtick about crappy energy infrastructure, which is that: the state fails miserably to build out any significant energy infrastructure, so at this point, Texas has more green energy of every type than California.

California has done plenty to harm the planet, while acting like it’s a paragon of virtue; it has lots and lots and lots of laws that make it look like it gives a sh\*t.

To that point, what, is stopping California from building its way out of this problem?

Why those very same laws. CEQA, a law that is supposed to preserve what really makes California great (naturally beautiful state) is a weapon of NIMBYs to stop anything from changing, including building affordable housing (yay homelessness) or green energy for the masses.

Today I walked by a house where lawn guys with 3 gas cars and a gas powered lawn mower were taking care of a house, that had solar and overpriced electric cars dotting the drivway.

And just as a cherry on top: there ain’t no water here in SoCal, but their lawn was looking mighty green.

Truly, that person is saving the planet.

Or maybe they’re just living in the land of make believe.

### Mexifornia

California, especially Southern California, has a very strong Mexican influence. I hear lots and lots of Spanish down here, see the Mexican flag proudly displayed on cars and homes, and went to a high school graduation where at least 95% of the graduating students were hispanic, just going off their first and last names.

It’s easy to cry foul and say “omg illegals” or “they took our jerbs”, but I see both sides. [Mexifornia](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1594030561?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback) lays it out very well, which is that, while many Mexicans or other hispanic groups are entering California, illegally or legally, a *lot* of them are living as though they are in Mexico. There is an odd lack of assimilation, of being skeptical of being American while benefitting from a country and state that are definitely a step up from Baja California.

A house across the way from us has 5 cars parked all around it. This is not a very large house, but clearly there are a huge number of people staying there. One of the cars was parked in a spot so long, it had cobwebs on it.

San Diego is 15 miles from the Mexican border. Tijuana is… not a place I’d move to any time soon. It’s got a bit of charm but it has that “don’t turn down this street” vibe.

What California suffers from is that it has people on both sides who believe (make believe?) that this large, hard working hispanic contingent is at their disposal. Companies and contractors (present company included!) benefit from these guys busting their asses to pick all our food, keep houses, lawns and yards going, and hold up California like Atlas holding up the Sky. They also benefit from the fact that California is *not* Mexico, though it might be sliding that way?

Folks on the left then count on this Mexifornia/Mexi-(state) contingent as a loyal voting base ([which Trump’s election showed is… not true](https://ssrs.com/insights/latino-male-voters-shift-toward-trump-in-2024-election/)), and so both sides are encouraged to keep the leaky border situation operating as it has.

Trump and his ICE thugs have decided that it’s time to enforce the laws, in ways that are both mean and bad. It is a reaction to this —

Those are the *apprehensions.* This doesn’t count the number of people, which when I studied this in school 15 years ago was 12 million illegals in the country. It’s at least that many now, so it’s somewhere around 5% of the population.

Democrats and Republicans pretend this situation is the other person’s fault, while both benefitting in different ways, and only individuals on the ground meet the consequences, like overcrowded schools (my daughter’s class is 27 kids) or abandoned vehicles, crime and lots and lots of trash.

Let’s continue to make believe the current state or ICE’s response are working though, so we can ignore the fact that it’s just creating another point of division in our society.

The book *Mexifornia* makes a very good point that, while this problem is most pronounced in California (of having an unassimilated, illegal immigrant population impinging on a social order), it is not *unique* to California; all the US southern border states have this issue and choose to deal with it differently (e.g., [Texas employs much more force at the state level](https://www.aclutx.org/news/texas-collaboration-with-ice-is-devastating-our-communities/)). California is not as much unique as it is the furthest along in this *transformation* into something not quite California, and not quite Mexico.

Speaking of division points, I’ll tie it off with my least favorite aspect of California, which is that, much like the rest of the USA, it’s a —

### Gerontocracy

Rule by the old. I also like “oldigarchy”, which I read about in [this book](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FR6NC5Z1?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback#averageCustomerReviewsAnchor) (Gerontocracy in America). The book has mixed reviews, but I think it drives home the point that there is an enormous concentration of wealth and power in the *elderly* in this country, especially in states like California, which reward incumbents ([Prop 13, Prop 19](https://www.empower.com/the-currency/life/california-proposition-13-proposition-19-what-to-know), and [crappy zoning](https://californialocal.com/localnews/statewide/ca/article/show/42216-zoning-laws-california-racial-segregation-development/)) and punish entrants.

A mostly accurate statement, both from moving here, and seeing who lives here is that: entrants == younger people, incumbents == old people.

If you bought a house in California in 1978, and still own it in 2022 (or if it was passed down via inheritance, where the tax rate is preserved), then [you’re looking at a disparity of ~300k](https://www.hjta.org/resources/taxpayer-tools/estimate-your-prop-13-savings/) in property taxes that you never had to pay, because your tax rate was locked at a lower value. You didn’t “earn” that as much as benefit from having a birthdate farther in the past.

Not a meritocracy. A gerontocracy.

But if you buy new, and aren’t using Prop 19 to move your tax rate with you, you just get your property reassessed and pay the new rate, which is punitive with the astronomical prices in (Southern) California.

The elderly here have done their very best to make sure that the property they own doesn’t become accessible to all but the wealthiest people, who are exceptionally high earners or other elderly.

I live in a neighborhood in San Diego that supposedly has [a median age of 48](https://www.aclutx.org/news/texas-collaboration-with-ice-is-devastating-our-communities/), but walking around, you’ll find an enormous concentration of elderly. California statistically speaking is relatively young, but I’ve found in my unfortunately anecdotal experience a lot of property owned by a very old population. The only people that are reasonably young here are all renters. The disparity on a [new home purchase vs. rental](https://www.axios.com/local/san-diego/2026/02/05/renting-cheaper-than-owning-san-diego-home) is thousands per month.

I started talking about gerontocracy and went to housing, but I think they’re pretty tightly coupled.

Old people benefit from fixed mortgage rates or paid off houses, especially if they are able to receive social security and medicare, while younger people, even economically proficient, have to fight over overpriced real estate and are punished by all sorts of taxes that the elderly don’t pay (Prop 13/19) or are the recipients of (Social Security and Medicare).

We can believe we’re in a meritocracy, but I don’t think merit is responsible for the state of things, as much as old people that passed laws, reinforced systems and zoned neighborhoods so that they’d have all the cards in their hands.

I find this one hard to believe, since the end result will be a much worse off society, whose vampirism only served to weaken the following generations to the point of [collapsing birth rates](https://www.macrotrends.net/global-metrics/countries/usa/united-states/birth-rate) and [increased rates of violence](https://rockinst.org/gun-violence/mass-shooting-factsheet/).

But maybe I’m just a doomer. Or maybe I just am ageist.

Anyway, I’ve got one last belief to talk about.

### Believing

One thing I’ve started to do regularly since being down here is attend a church. Among all the *make believe* I’ve found in California, church is one of the obvious “hey this is all made up” (says the atheist) or “this is the literal word of god” (says the devout believer). I wouldn’t put myself in either camp at this point, though I was in the Secular Student Alliance at one point (lol).

There are lots of great things to see at a church. Budding families, people who want to better their lives, people serving to help kids and adults through hard times, like the [RFK camp](https://www.forthechildrensd.org/).

I don’t think I’m communing with god, definitely not after slogging through the Old Testament and rereading the New Testament (if anything, I just have *more* questions after reading the source text, not fewer).

Belief for me is an act. It’s going to church, singing the kids songs my daughter learns at Sunday school, listening to the sermon, talking it over with my wife. I have to make my beliefs, they don’t just come from within.

And on reflection of the things above that bother so much about California, I think it’s because *I make myself focus on these things, while doing nothing about them*. Because while homelessness, faux environmentalism, Mexifornia and gerontocracy live in my head rent free, I don’t do s — t to try and fix them, even though solutions exist even at the individual level. It’s easier for me to b\*tch and moan about it instead of suiting up and trying to fix any of the aspects of it.

I think writing this is more of an exorcism of vile feelings than anything productive. Belief for me is an act, and since I am acting like these things are important while simultaneously doing nothing about them, maybe I should sweep clean the demons in my head that plague me about this state.

Maybe I should instead focus on the good, on the walking commute to my daughter’s school, where her lunches are paid for and she’s getting special help from the staff, where there’s public transit that works, even if it has its problems, and where there are the most beautiful sunsets I’ve seen in a long time. There are things that are bad about California, but as long as I choose to live in it, I should focus on what makes living down here worth living.

Or at the very least, I can *believe* it’s worth living here.
