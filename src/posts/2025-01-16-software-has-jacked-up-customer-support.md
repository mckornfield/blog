I consider myself a relatively patient person when it comes to dealing with logistics like a support hotline. I once forgot my treasurydirect.gov password AND messed up one of my security questions, so I sat on hold for 1–2 hours to talk to someone about resetting it.

BUT my patience has run out when it comes to the s\*\*t that is foisted on consumers, no, *customers* who are trying to get help for something they’re paying for.

It’s all because of an over-reliance on software.

Here are my (and many others) grievances.

### Press 0 to Actually Get Some Real Help

Call trees are one of the worst thing I have had to deal with in my life time. And making it voice based is ultimately worse, since I have to wait longer to use the touch tones that actually work.

The reality is that all call trees are inherently terrible pieces of software. They rely on a system that’s got a crappy interface (your voice, or a 9 digit setup), and up front they produce information that you almost never need (no, I’m not calling to check my balance, are you *serious*).

Recently a call tree offered to send me a link to an app. In some sense, that would be great if the app offered me any support, but in another sense, it’s just annoying and redundant.

You know what might be helpful? Instead of investing in more call tree software and apps, just hiring more people to answer the phone or a chat. What a concept. But I’ll get to why companies won’t do that.

### Chat Bot Apps are Useless

Let me summarize every conversation I’ve ever had with a chatbot:

*Me: “I need help with …”*

*Chatbot: “I see that you’re asking about your account, here are fifteen options you don’t care about…”*

*Me: “Speak to a customer service representative”*

*Chatbot: “I can see you want to speak to a customer service representative, let me put you in touch with a support specialist.”*

*Me: “Thank God.”*

I’ve never, *never*, gotten something useful from a chatbot. I have an idea of why they exist, which I’ll get to below, but at this point in my usage of them, they are just way crummier forms of ChatGPT.

### I Know Why Companies Have Done This

It’s two things:

1. Companies like to act like they are efficiently handling cases, so they deploy a bunch of redundant processes to appear helpful
2. Companies don’t give a s\*\*t about customers, and burying actual help behind many walls will dissuade all but the most patient/committed victims (and save them from having to hire more *actual people*)

There’s this idea that software just *fixes* problems in all cases, but I’d argue in *most* cases, software just introduces new problems.

If you have a crappy support process, that’s understaffed, and slap 10 tons of software on it, you’ll have an *expensive*, [*dark pattern riddled*](https://www.deceptive.design/)system that every person who uses it hates.

Software isn’t the problem, but using it as the solution has just f — ked things up.

### What You Should Do Instead

Here’s a recipe for surviving this nonsense, that corporations are stupidly behind

1. Pick the system that lets you speak to a person as quickly as possible, meaning usually a phone or live chat
2. Give yourself something to do in case you’re stuck on hold
3. Have a clear script of what you need accomplished (I need to dispute this bill, etc.)
4. Don’t fill out the survey, they clearly don’t give a crap about your experience, because they’ve already hosed it

### What Should Companies Do?

I don’t think anyone is listening to me, but good support looks like this

1. Good process
2. Good people
3. Software that helps 1 and 2

Right now it’s

1. Crap process
2. Not enough people
3. Software that makes 1 and 2 worse

Lord help us users, we’re going to need it while we’re on hold.
