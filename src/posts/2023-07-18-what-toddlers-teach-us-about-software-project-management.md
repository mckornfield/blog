I recently had a rough time getting some family photos taken with my two-year-old and realized it had a very strong correlation with a lot of things I’ve seen in mismanaged projects.

In the span of one hour, we went from a happy family to aggravated parents stuffing a screaming toddler into a car seat.

In the span of weeks or months, bright-eyed developers become angry developers, cramming garbage into barely functional infrastructure to meet a deadline.

How do things go so wrong? Is it normal?

### Make sure to build in time after whatever you’re doing

**Toddlers:** Assume you’re going to do something, *anything*, with a toddler.

- You need time afterward to get them back in the car or to get them in the stroller.
- You have to leave time to deal with their big emotions and big feelings.
- You need time to put their shoes and clothes on again after they take them all off (sigh)

Toddlers are not on your schedule, and putting them on your schedule just heightens the emotional response for all parties.

I wanted to get back in time for a meeting I set, but really what I needed to do was say, “I’m going to have to miss the meeting time. Can we bump it 30 minutes?” and give my kid the time she needed to play on the playground.

**Project Management:** At the tactical level, this matters quite a bit. Stacking back-to-back meetings doesn’t give people time to breathe (or, if in person, actually make it to the meeting on time).

Giving 5–10 minutes for people to breathe is not just smart but necessary when scheduling meetings as part of a project. And meetings will run over, so giving space for that means people don’t feel constantly rushed around like a toddler being dragged somewhere.

At the strategic level, this extra time means that the “go live” date should be proceeded by things like a “stealth release” or “beta” with much lower stakes.

The sorts of things that can go wrong in a software release are myriad, but trying to shake them all out in a short or non-existent time window means they’ll pile up, and you won’t be sure what the exact issue is. Leaving what folks call “integration” time is the best way to ensure strategic success for a project.

### Getting mad or getting aggressive only makes things worse

**Toddlers** match your energy. I was frustrated because I had somewhere to be, and my frustration was matched by anger and frustration on the part of the screaming little one.

In some ways, I had always thought that’s how you dealt with kids, as a battle of wills. I’d seen it acted out by my own parents (more my dad and stepdad than my mom), and I’d seen it in movies, etc.

But I don’t think it buys us much as parents or as humans. In certain life or death situations, sure, drag the screaming toddler away from the cliff.

Most of the time, I think you’re just building resentment on both sides and even if you have to take the toddler out of a situation, being less angry/aggressive can make the whole experience much better.

**Project Management:** I’ve been in projects where I’ve been yelled at for things not being done on time/not getting worked through. I’ve seen it happen to other people as well. One of the main differences between toddlers and folks in IT is their personalities are more introspective, so yelling at them rarely turns into a screaming match (also, they understand what “At Will Hire/Fire” means).

Instead, resentment builds.

What I have seen happen is resentment leads to two things: disinterest and a lack of loyalty. Disinterested teammates don’t want to make a better product; they want to pad their resume and move on to the next big project, either inside the company or outside. They’ll get something done, sure, but with no regard for the project's long-term success.

Getting angry with people over project deadlines makes things worse. The team will want the project to end as soon as possible, and to make sure never to work with whoever yelled at them again, if they can help it.

Sure, yelling at the team in a meeting might have gotten “things done,” but just like dragging the screaming toddler somewhere, was it really worth it?

## It’s Not Worth It

You have precious time with your toddler and very little time with the projects you have in your life. All you’ll have after that time are memories of the feelings you had, not the individual things you did.

Do you want your memories to be of screaming, angry toddlers, or angry project managers? I don’t think so. Given that everything could be the same, but the screaming and anger are removed, I think we’d have much better memories.

Build in time before and after, keep a cool head, and understand that time management is hard, so you don’t end up making things worse than they already are.
