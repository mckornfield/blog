### Success

In the world of business, success means you have customers. People that are willing to pay you for a product. Pretty obvious, yeah?

The difference between a government entity and a business organization is that government entities are guaranteed monopolies. The only way to take your business elsewhere is to physically move outside the state/country.

Businesses can be monopolistic but hopefully the customers can go to more than one place to get a product.

In *From Zero to One*, Peter Thiel argues that most good software companies are monopolies. They are able to use some secrets (i.e. software that solves a problem that is not open source) and make serious money off of that secret, cornering a whole market.

- Google’s search engine and associated ads product is one example.
- Microsoft Windows Desktop is another. (75% of desktop devices)
- The iPhone and iOS is probably a good third example (currently 56% of the US market, so a slight majority more than a monopoly)

These monopolies or very strong market captures are great for companies. They give them a ton of revenue, a large customer base, and disposible capital to invest in innovation.

But do they innovate? I don’t really think so.

### The Cost of Success

Success brings with it a lot of dollars for people at the top (and some for those at the bottom, sure), but the real cost of success is *reliability.*

Windows had some major muck-ups with releases like Vista, that showed just how important reliability is. [With a buggy and crummy release](http://paulwallbank.com/2012/07/06/how-much-did-vista-really-cost-microsoft/), Microsoft let Apple grow its meagre desktop business into the second largest in the world, by a wide margin over any other competitors besides Microsoft.

When you have lots of customers, you also have lots of people who expect your product to continue working the way it always has. “Innovations” usually come at a high cost, and one of them is angry customers who are willing to flee.

The cost of success is that reliability becomes MUCH more important than innovation. I’d argue that all of the innovations that the tech companies have developed (like Amazon/AWS, Microsoft and Google) over their rise have largely been innovations of reliability.

Google Search got a lot faster, more accurate, and more enriched over time. It’s not a [fundamentally new product](http://web.archive.org/web/20010621202639/http://www.google.com/), but one that runs on a much more complicated system than it initially did, and one that is so reliable that it’s used by many people to check their internet connection.

But reliability is a pretty narrow form of innovation.

Making a house stand up to a hurricane is not the same as making a house at the bottom of the ocean or the surface of the moon.

Google’s innovations after search, like Chrome, Gmail, Maps and Android, became huge products in their own right, but very quickly became part of the more rigid/ harder to change parts of the company. They had graduated from innovations to “legacy code,” which had to be maintained, upgraded and made to work with all sorts of devices and systems that customers used.

Apple primarily gets away from this by forcing obsolescence into their products. Trying to run the latest iOS on an iPhone from 5 years ago is impossible. But their innovations since the iPhone have largely been accessories or a big iPhone (the iPad). Otherwise they have made less buggy, faster versions of the same thing over the last 15 years.

### Victims of Our Own Success

The dark side of success is that a lot of steps taken to make a reliable, robust product are not necessarily awesome decisions. And the culture that produced a product is never perfect, but it is very hard to change a company that has been doing things successfully, even if some of the things that it does aren’t right.

I used to work somewhere that had pretty great company culture, but the culture around unit testing was pretty non-existent. Many folks would join the company, see the crazy slow integration tests from years before, and get confused as to why things weren’t simply unit tested.

But as the company added customers and the number of employees, why change the way we did things? My boss at the time told me that because we’d become so successful doing things a certain way, we had become “victims of our own success.”

Even if a company has a great culture with almost no problems, making changes to the system is now a much larger risk to the success of a company. And changing software can result in unknown breakages as the system becomes more complex, meaning a more intensive regression suite as well as a more rigorous release process are required for making the same sorts of changes that a startup might make.

This is what economists Thaler and Sunstein would call “Sludge”, from their book *Nudge*. Sludge makes it harder to do things, like change or innovate, almost by design.

### The Agility of Startups

Startups have agility really for one reason:

1. They don’t have customers

Having customers is great because you have money coming in. But you also have to support those customers and the ways they do things, leading to the problem of making sure the house can survive a coming storm, vs. building the house on the moon (to reuse an earlier analogy).

Building a house on the moon doesn’t serve any customers, because no customers are there yet. But it is definitely an innovation that would require a lot of new technology and patterns to get something figured out. And once customers *did* get there, you’d be the only property owner on the moon!

That’s essentially what gives startups the freedom to experiment and “fail fast.” They don’t have to support millions of queries per second, or millions of daily active users. Their main user is themselves and a small circle that (hopefully) builds around them.

And the smaller the circle, the faster startups can innovate, because they can be laser focused on the moon house instead of the falling apart regular house that a customer is living in.
