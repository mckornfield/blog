[Suno](https://suno.com) is one of a few “AI music production” platforms that has come on the scene. It seemed decent when I tried it out about six months ago, but found that just prompting a generic song was… just as generic as I expected.

Not too bad sounding, but not going to win me a Grammy anytime soon.

Things changed when my friend started sharing his AI generated music with me. It was both well executed AND had his voice in the writing, because he wrote the lyrics.

He was also very good at prompting the right styles and shared with me a few of his secrets for getting the music to sound right. I was hooked.

I want to talk through my thoughts on the platform, how I’ve used it, and how I don’t think it’s a real threat to live music, but maybe to other types of music.

### Suno as a business

Suno is a part of AI’s push to make “thoughtwork” cheaper. What would take audio engineers months/weeks/days to create can be done in minutes or hours using this tool or other AI music equivalents.

This is not to say these things are equivalent in terms of quality, but the day may come where they *are* equivalent.

On the other side of things, Suno and friends will have to grapple with the reality of where its models [get their abilities to generate music](https://www.theguardian.com/music/article/2024/jun/25/record-labels-sue-ai-song-generator-apps-copyright-infringement-lawsuit) but given that it can [overcome those issues](http://Suno%27s%202026%20model%20will%20replace%20its%20existing%20version%20and%20will%20require%20users%20to%20pay%20for%20audio%20downloads,%20said%20Warner), it’s a matter of time before the latest and greatest way to create prototypes or finished products of music is with AI driven music production.

That being said, Taylor and Beyonce probably have little to worry about, people still want line performances by ultra talented individuals who can sing, dance and captivate.

**Winners and Losers**

I think Suno is disruptive to *electronic musicians*, or people who are upstream of live performances. It will allow poets or novice musicians to create decent sounds/songs with much less technical know-how.

[Here’s one example of an AI artist](https://www.forbes.com/sites/dougmelville/2025/09/27/al-singer-xania-monet-just-charted-on-billboard-signed-3m-deal-is-this-the-future-of-music/) taking off, but obviously she’s not a live performer. But [she’s not alone](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/nov/13/ai-music-spotify-billboard-charts). And it’s one thing to say “well this is all just computer nonsense!” but a whole other thing to consider that it’s a tool for making something in the hands of capable people, which results in something that people *want to listen to*.

The live performers who take hold of this tooling I think will be *more* empowered to do things like:

- Prototype their songs
- Cover their songs into other genres (one simple feature of Suno)
- Allow for really rapid iteration with lyrics
- Experimenting with different instruments and sounds on an album

The losers are unfortunately those involved with the status quo of music production: people who are using Digital Audio Workstations (DAWs) and their equivalents to make music and beats, and who think AI music is just slop or a fad.

As the field becomes filled with people with other talents (lyric writing, prompt engineering haha), I believe it will “democratize” (for better and for worse) the music production space. I believe those who are already in the music industry that embrace these tools and work around their shortcomings will be the next uber successful musician/producers.

### My Suno Experience

I am a fan of certain artists’ styles, so I went for making an emo album and an easy listening/pop rock album. My main input was writing the lyrics, inspired by some things that had happened to me or family. I don’t consider myself much of a lyricist or poet, but this loop was pretty great for me:

1. Come up with a poem/song, write it in 10ish minutes (basically a total rough draft)
2. Come up with a style I want (these were “lo-fi, chill hops”)
3. Tweak the weirdness and style influence knobs
4. (Optional) If I’m particularly inspired, I’ll sing what I sort of think the chorus should sound like in my head. I’ve only done this for a few
5. Hit Create
6. Listen
7. Tweak both lyrics, style and the “knobs”
8. Create again until I feel like it’s “good enough”

This all takes on the order of an hour or so a song to get something that I consider “OK”, good is probably a few more hours, usually after waiting and listening to it within the context of an album that I’m trying to put together.

After this, if I feel like I have something “worthy”, then I graduate it to Spotify with Distrokid. Weirdly, there is no place where I have to say that the music is “made with AI”, but I do have to put the artists’ name in, for which I just put the persona.

### It’s All About Taste

I’m relatively early in my journey with these tools, but I think the defining characteristic of using them is “taste.”

Knowing what sounds bad and what sounds good, what someone would actually want to listen to vs. what *I* want to listen to, is something that I struggle with. I like the sound of my own music because I like those styles and I recognize the lyrics (since I wrote them), but… I doubt they have mass market appeal.

All I know is that there are 3 axes to this music discussion:

- Quality
- Voice
- Liveness

Suno really only lives on two axes: you can ramp up the **quality** by using its more extensive editing tools or dialing in your prompts/Personas/Covers, but **voice** only comes through well if you inject your own sound or lyrics in. It has these interesting hooks you can create (short videos) to try and embrace the **liveness** aspect, but at the end of the day, the music is generated by machines, even if the inputs are human.

I think people, generally speaking, want quality music, with some unique voice, and to top it off a *live experience*. I believe that what made bands like Linkin Park or Nickelback successful is their unique *voice* mixed with quality execution, that you can buy a ticket to see. (I know putting them next to each other is weird…)

But even if I create a Suno album that maxes out the sound of my “voice” and is decent quality, I won’t be able to perform it live.

And so Taylor and Beyonce will still be rad musicians and I’ll just be some dude trying to make some tunes.
