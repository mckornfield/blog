In the workplace, I worked with people who were “Product Owners”, but was confused when I looked at the workspace portal and found that they were referred to as “Product Managers.”

When I asked about this, the person I asked (who was a Product Manager) said

> Well PO is a Role, but PM is a Title.

I think fatherhood functions similarly. Let me explain.

### Fatherhood as a Title: Father

A title is something you wear but *don’t necessarily do.* This can be a dad who is in the household but always has his interests elsewhere outside of his kids.

It can be a divorced dad, or a dad who dropped off his sperm and then stayed the hell away.

These people are still “fathers”, in the sense that they have fathered children, but their emotional involvement is low to nothing.

Their children probably refer to them as “father” or don’t know they exist.

“Fatherhood” is a title, and often it can come with almost no upside. Tense, critical conversations, followed by being completely ignored. Fathers are absentee generals, ready to throw their boys into the line of fire without even looking up from their papers/phones, but quick to tell them what they need to do better. Being around them is an exercise in pulling teeth.

Fatherhood is a title, nothing more.

### Fatherhood as a Role: Dad

This is taking kids to practices, playing games with them, going for walks, getting involved at school, feeding and changing. Taking an interest in what your kid is interested in. Being force to play in the pool too long.

You know, kid stuff!

But it means you’re getting in on the action and building an emotional base for your kid. They have someone to trust and that models what an “adult male” looks like.

Scott Galloway writes about this better than I will in *Notes on Being a Man*, but in his book, he compares his father (who really embodied the “title” of father) to what he does for his boys and what he encourages others to do. This consists of (but is not limited to):

- Spending garbage time with kids (just doing errands)
- Doing things the *kids* are interested in doing
- Sharing your interests with kids, understanding they might not give a dang
- Showing, not telling, how to be a man

A dad is a role.

### My Dads

I had my biological dad (I call him “dad”, despite the way this article is written haha) and my step dad, who I call “Joe” (because that’s his name). Joe did many things differently than my dad did, as they are very different people, but Joe was present in my life as a kid, in a way that he didn’t have to be as my step dad.

Joe filled the “dad” role for me, whereas my own dad slipped farther and farther into the “father” role as I learned more about the way [he conducted himself](https://mckornfield.medium.com/a-letter-to-my-dad-be06a7f609d8). I have a ton in common with my dad, but I like to think that I’ve (I am really just a bald version of him)

This isn’t to shame my dad; he’s had to deal with a lot of things (some of his own design), but to rather praise my step dad.

For all his faults, Joe was the “dad” role that I needed growing up. I attribute my affection for classic rock, being out on the water, and outdoor barbecue to the man. Most of all, he makes me appreciate men who take on burdens that they *don’t have to.*

But boys need men like this.

### Find Your Dad/ Seek Out Your Apprentice

If you’re a person whose “father” isn’t around, that stinks. But there are men out there who are willing to mentor you and show you what it means to be a man. They could be a coach, a teacher, a church member, or an uncle or someone outside one’s nuclear family.

Seeking them can be hard, and they may be in short supply, but I know that the embodied male mentors I’ve had in my life were worth a million Joe Rogan and Jordan Peterson podcast episodes.

On the supply side, men have to step up and act as dads/older brothers to folks who haven’t had a fair shake. Ideally for their own sons (looking at you, fathers!), but hopefully they can create some of that *surplus value* that Scott Galloway talks about, and help mentor other boys/young men.

There but for the grace of god go many, many boys who need a mentor that’s not ignoring them, saying drivel on social media, or criticizing them. Someone grounded who has an income, has a partner, and heck, maybe even children. Someone who can show them light, not darkness.

Big Brothers Big Sisters is a good way to give back, as well as just showing up in community activities like your church or volunteering.

What boys need is more people in the dad role to show them the way, and fewer fathers with their empty titles, criticizing them from afar.
