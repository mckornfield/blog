I recently watched the [Bernie Madoff documentary on Netflix](https://www.netflix.com/title/81466159) and encourage you to do so if you have a few hours to spare on understanding the world’s largest Ponzi Scheme.

The title of the piece is a bit tongue in cheek (once you watch the documentary), but it’s also something than many people think and believe: Bernie Madoff was a monster that took advantage of poor, unsuspecting investors. He duped the SEC and thousands of people who poured 19 billion dollars into his “hedge fund.”

I felt little sympathy for the people harmed in the documentary. Wall Street is not your friend. [It’s a cut-throat, make money or lose it world](https://medium.com/r?url=https%3A%2F%2Fwww.youtube.com%2Fwatch%3Fv%3DQqjQxs9l5fg).

But the documentary reveals some flaws in the system, flaws that won’t be solved by more or less regulation, or better ethics. The biggest flaw I think it points out is…

### The Lies We Tell Ourselves

More than anything, what kept Madoff alive was the lying. It wasn’t just the lies he told all of his investors; those are the obvious.

The less obvious lie was that this man could beat the market so consistently. They had lied to *themselves*.

Many investment books will tell you: [there is no way to beat the market](https://fourminutebooks.com/a-random-walk-down-wall-street-summary/). Warren Buffet famously beat hedge funds by [running a bet and just investing in one indexed fund](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/investing/030916/buffetts-bet-hedge-funds-year-eight-brka-brkb.asp#:~:text=In%202008%2C%20Warren%20Buffett%20issued,Bloomberg%20op%2Ded%20in%20May.), i.e. *the market*.

The documentary states that Bernie Madoff was a man who’s father had a bunch of failed business ventures. He dropped out of Law School, not because he was too smart, but because he couldn’t cut it. This is the man who is beating every investor on Wall Street, the man with the crystal ball?

And the fact that he kept his successes so under wraps just feeds the lie even more. He’s not only the best, but also humble? When people are [beating the market, they tell *everyone*](https://www.yahoo.com/video/bridgewater-flagship-hedge-fund-beat-205353558.html?guccounter=1&guce_referrer=aHR0cHM6Ly93d3cuZ29vZ2xlLmNvbS8&guce_referrer_sig=AQAAALa5Jx0-_HJH7bM8DQh8hxNeKEgLKnnXgLwTkfRCK5kJdU4h5NYDExNQUpIX2oPJWqP0nueMV_j6ru-R45v_pFa2119m9rdW-kaK1JGPhVEwTssPJgoQIoBGG_7lMrj_O0ycRT3NpnNcZ1OgkW0h2k1k6o8NgA-eayKr5yjZiIp8).

What his investors loved was the idea that they could make so much money. They fell in love with the story that Madoff fabricated on his infamous *17th floor* about how their money was just growing and growing, no matter what happened to the market.

They trusted Madoff and the SEC, the narrative that these were trustworthy groups… This brings me to my next point, the biggest criminal after Madoff himself was

### The SEC

The Securities Exchange Commission, or SEC, was founded after the 1929 market crash. Their stated goal is to “protect investors in the $3.8 trillion municipal securities markets that cities and towns rely on.”

Sure. Good job in 2008 by the way. 👏👏👏

Between Madoff and the subprime crisis, they really showed us how much protecting they do.

What they really are is much more confusing. The SEC is a group that legitimizes Wall Street as “government sanctioned.” But in reality, the SEC is something that is *Wall Street sanctioned*.

When the SEC hits Wall Street with fines, or “audits” these companies, they pay the fines or they sit through the audits, but it’s all theater.

[The SEC is a revolving door](https://www.cnbc.com/2012/08/16/the-truth-about-the-sec-and-wall-street.html); the folks that work in the SEC are either coming from or going to positions within Wall Street. [Madoff himself was on SEC advisory committees and the chairman of the NASDAQ board](https://fortune.com/2021/04/15/how-ponzi-king-bernie-madoff-conned-investors-and-seduced-regulators/).

The documentary points out that the SEC failed multiple times to uncover Madoff’s scheme, not even bother to call a clearinghouse to see if his transactions were legitimate. They ignored multiple attempts to contact them from [a member of the industry providing evidence that what he was doing was impossible](https://www.npr.org/2010/03/02/124208012/madoff-whistleblower-sec-failed-to-do-the-math).

At one point one of the heads of the SEC called Madoff and asked if he ran a hedge fund. He said no. That was the end of the story. That person just *trusted* him.

The documentary even points out that Madoff’s company gained credibility since he had a “clean bill of health” from the SEC. Oof.

What the SEC lacks most is real motivation to actually police Wall Street. They are hand and glove with the institution that they police.

If they actually *did their jobs*, I would bet Wall Street would place someone in the Federal Government to dismantle the SEC.

The best way for the SEC to survive is if it *appears* to police Wall Street, but doesn’t actually do anything.

Government institutions simply want to exist and be stable. To gain more contracts and more employees. To have more power, or at least appear to do so. Ruffling feathers is a great way to get your whole institution defunded.

Failing specularly and then promising to do better is a great way to grow. We’ll get ‘em next time guys!

When a corporation fails to do something, people tend to get fired. When a government agency fails to do something, [they need more training, more resources, more special units](https://www.sec.gov/spotlight/secpostmadoffreforms.htm).

Since 2008 the [SEC has added about 1000 employees](https://www.heritage.org/sites/default/files/inline-images/BG-Reforming-the-SEC-Chart-page1-825.gif).

Or take the Department of Homeland Security, an organization that didn’t exist before 9/11, and is now home to a [quarter of a million employees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Homeland_Security).

If something really bad goes down, you can bet there will be more government employees to “keep us safe.” For sure.

### Why I Don’t Think Madoff was a Monster

This gets to my final point. It’s so easy to blame one man. One evil man with evil henchmen and henchwomen that concocted a scheme that fooled thousands of people into giving him 20 billion dollars.

He loved his family. He treated his friends and relatives well. He did a lot in a supposed “giving” manner. He was much more complicated than a simple monster.

But he also lied to that family every day, for years and years.

He had big investors that definitely knew something was off, but decided to be part of the fun. He wasn’t the only “monster” around.

The fact is that the monster that was Madoff relied on a system that was exploitable, a system built on *trust*.

- Trust in your government institutions to say if something is legitimate.
- Trust in your family, to not lie to you and to do the right thing.
- Trust in you as a person, when you produce facts and figures from fabricated trading programs on dot matrix paper, that those represent real trades.

I think the real monster in this system is the reliance on t*rust*.

- You can’t trust the government; they are incompetent, incentivized to lie and work with corporations who seek to take from you.
- You can’t trust your family; they can be monsters just as much as anyone else.
- You can’t trust an investment company CEO; he could be a crook looking to take your money and buy himself a house in France and a jet.

We live in a world where billions of people interact in a way where it is impossible to know who is lying or telling the truth.

We aren’t in tribal societies where you know everyone by name. (And even then, people can lie.)

We live in the era of *no trust.* Or as software security folks say, *zero trust.*

### So Is There Any Hope?

The software world has shown me that nothing is sacred, nothing is to be trusted. All things within a software system must prove some sort of shared “truth” in order to gain access.

But that said, all systems can be exploited, including software systems. The only antidote to that is [that systems and standards must be open](https://www.mend.io/resources/blog/3-reasons-why-open-source-is-safer-than-commercial-software/), so that anyone can observe and attempt to fix them.

I think if the SEC or Wall Street want to operate in a fashion that is redeemable, that gives some respectability to the system, they should adopt standards and technology so that all Americans can audit and review the ways that banks operate.

I’d imagine a system like crypto’s blockchain, or something a bit more purpose-fit for Wall Street’s myriad of transactions, should exist. One that doesn’t rely on trust to determine if people are following the law.

Not more audits, more government oversight, more meetings and delays. Not relying on corrupt watchmen. Not relying on paper laws and whether or not they are enforced by ideologues.

I’m no Wall Street investor, so I’ll speculate here:

Imagine a world where in order to trade on Wall Street you instead trade on a system where all transactions are viewable by everyone, in an audit trail that can be reviewed and held to a set of automatic standards.

Let’s call it the *no trust market.*

A world where you can’t just relabel or rebundle securities and get them signed off by a “ratings agency” (this is a knock at Subprime).

A world where you can’t report numbers to investors by printing fake things and mailing them (what Madoff did).

Where truth lives in a distributed system that is viewable by everyone. The SEC doesn’t have to do some audit to check if numbers in one place match another. The system ensures that is the case. The laws are codified in the system that everyone must use in order to transact on Wall Street. A system that doesn’t let us lie to ourselves.

And maybe in this system, if you do something fishy, if the numbers DON’T match, there are automatic checks in the system that flag you as a “schemer” or “criminal.” Maybe those checks immediately prevent you from trading, and return your funds to your investors.

No need for you to turn yourself in to the SEC.

No need for the SEC in the first place.

Would Wall Street ever agree to such a system? Maybe not. But I think the reality is, in order to survive in the no trust world, we need to adopt such systems. We need to accept that trust is dead, and something else must take its place. And that something else is not more SEC employees.

An open system that enforces the laws automatically and not based on competency is what we need.
