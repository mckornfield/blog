### The Rewired Brain

I’m part way through Dr Ski Chilton’s book [The Rewired Brain](https://www.amazon.com/ReWired-Brain-Yourself-Negative-Behaviors/dp/080100747X), and so far it’s a decent read. The book centers around how the two classic systems of your brain, System 1 and System 2, interact.

- System 1 -> Fast, unconscious, difficult to directly control
- System 2 -> Slow, conscious, easier to control

In one of the chapters, *“*Your Brain on Change”, the metaphor of highways really stuck with me.

Your brain is one of the most complicated feedback loop systems ever conceived. But what you can liken it to is a highway. The more onramps there are to negative, destructive thinking, the more cars (thoughts) that move onto the highway, and the more difficult it will be to live a healthy life.

My personal problems are all in the realm of negative self talk, things like:

- You’re not worth it
- Why even try it’s just going to break/fail
- You’re so stupid

The book asks you to try and see what in your life is responsible for these negative System 1 onramps, and for me I think it’s down to two things I want to talk out, in case it resonates with anyone: crushes and dads.

This will be part advice to my past and present self, part unpacking why I might have built these super highways to begin with.

### Crushes on Women

I think years ago this would have been embarrassing for me to write about, but now it’s more of a cautionary tale. I do think this is a big part of those negative thoughts that have built up over time is how I handled having crushes on women.

I think everyone’s had a crush at some point: you like someone but feel powerless to do anything about it. You act out a lot of wishful thinking and wonder why the person won’t spontaneously like you.

My crushes were all the same through grade school:

- Pick a pretty girl to have a crush on
- Do nothing except an awkward stare or pretend indifference
- Wonder why the girl was never interested in me
- Feel worthless

In retrospect, it wasn’t all bad. My wife tells me I saved myself a good deal of heartache not dating until college, and I can’t imagine pursuing all of these girls would have gotten me anywhere.

But that second step in the crush process is the worst part, because it really feeds the System 1 thinking. By not doing anything but still having strong feelings for someone, you have the lingering sense that something is *wrong*. And when something appears wrong, your brain gets to work solving the problem:

- She doesn’t like me because I’m ugly
- I must be so stupid she won’t even take the time to talk to me
- I’m worth less than the gum on her shoe

And you know, maybe all of those things are true! But the reality is probably more along the lines of: she’s her own person, and if you don’t try to interact with her, you’re asking for a miracle as far as relationships go.

Crushes got way better for me in college, because I just decided to ask people out. And boy was it awkward since I had no practice at it. And I got rejected I think 95% of the time.

But it was actually *great* for my self-esteem. The lingering wondering is just asking for System 1 to grow into a monster you’ll spend a lot of time wrestling with later.

In another book I’ve read recently: *From Zero to One*, Peter Thiel says:

> Sometimes you do have to fight. Where that’s true, you should fight and win. There is no middle ground: either don’t throw any punches, or strike hard and end it quickly.

That’s my advice on how to handle your crushes. But just staring at your crush isn’t the same as not throwing any punches, you’re constantly focusing your attention and powering feedback loops in your brain, so you’re in this state of a “half-measure,” to use the lingo from [Breaking Bad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_Measures). And half-measures never work.

So if you’re like me, and you can’t get your attention away from the person, then strike hard and end it quickly:

- *strike* up a conversation
- find out what they’re interested in
- ask them if they’d like to do something with you that matches their interests

If you lose (rejection), great, now those lingering feelings will move away. At least they always did for me. What’s the point in having a crush on a person if you know they don’t want to spend time with you?

**tl;dr** Ask out your crushes as soon as you can, and don’t let lingering your feelings for them fester. Strike hard and end it quickly.

### Daddy issues

I don’t have a good relationship wth my dad, and as someone who’s now a daughter of a two year old, I feel introspecting *why* my relationship with him isn’t good might help me both be a good dad *and* attack my System 1 highway problem.

Like many men, my dad is a narcissist. He’s also not one that you can as easily identify, but here are some telltale signs:

- He invites you to things *he* wants to do
- If things aren’t convenient for him, they won’t get done
- The world is always being crappy to him

Narcissism is a toxic trait. I think the main reason he’s that way is that he’s partially autistic, but I also think he’s the oldest child and the “golden boy,” the kid who could do no wrong.

But my dad has done a lot of crummy things, some that are borderline criminal, and other things that are just mean. But I’m not seeking to trash him here, mainly to give context to why I think my negative self talk is fed pretty heavily by him.

Dads are supposed to be one of two pillars of trust you build your life on. You have your mom as a given, who [for many people (40%?)](https://www.pewresearch.org/fact-tank/2022/03/11/rising-share-of-americans-see-women-raising-children-on-their-own-cohabitation-as-bad-for-society/#:~:text=The%20share%20of%20births%20to,somewhat%20by%20race%20and%20ethnicity.) is the only person they can rely on. Dads are supposed to be this bonus figure, they step in and help in ways that maybe mom can’t, or take over when mom is exhausted from the crazy amount of mental and physical tax kids can have on your life. Dads can’t breastfeed but they can complement moms pretty well, being a source of play and having different interests/ desires.

There are three types of dads in my view:

1. Obviously there’s the devoted loving father who’s there for the long haul, sticking it out through rough times in a marriage and life so that they can be their for their kids.
2. There’s the dad dads do absolutely nothing. They drop their sperm off and they leave the child raising to a mom and possibly another man who might come into her life. I always thought these men were the worst, not taking responsibility for their offspring, and in a lot of ways, they do suck. But the worst category of men is:
3. Dads who do the bare minimum (every other weekend warriors), and assume they’re “doing they’re part” and “being a good dad.” Or they show up and give a teddy bear or some game.

Kids don’t want that stuff. They don’t want to be whisked off to some strange place they don’t know, or put in a place they’re uncomfortable. They don’t want some toy from a man they have no good emotional connection with. Kids want trust and connection, and you build that by spending time with them where they feel comfortable and supported.

When you are only somewhat involved in your kid’s life, and you don’t build a layer of trust for them, you can help invite in things like that negative self-talk (at least that’s what happened to me). I don’t think my nephew is any different; I think he has a lot of self-negativity that comes from a dad that didn’t gave him the quality time he deserved.

I said half-measures always fail, and with dads it’s no different.

If I had any advice for my dad 20+ years ago, when my parents divorced, it would have been:

> Either be the best dad to me you can, or don’t show up.

What did he do? Moved away from us for a while. Eventually he had his own apartment and made a space for us, but once he remarried, we (my brother and I) were always strangers in his house and his space.

The best memory I have is when I went on a trip with him to the Grand Canyon. I didn’t feel like I was just along for the ride, that was a decent time. Also the Grand Canyon is pretty hard to beat, when you see it for the first time.

But so much of my time with him was low trust, high anxiety situations. I remember a ski trip I was so anxious about, I ducked him on it last minute and got scolded about it. I definitely added that to the pile of “Matt is not good enough for his dad.”

When you’re a kid, you don’t have an adult brain. You can’t process the fact that someone doesn’t care about you or would act purely in their own self interest, especially if that person is your parent. So your System 1 brain steps in: “well they’re your loving and nurturing parent, so *you* must be the problem.” And so the highway onramps get added to.

The crappy half-assed version of a person is not something any kid wants. And not something any kids need.

**tl;dr** Dads, be good or be gone. The best advice I can give to a kid: if you’re uncomfortable being around the person, advocate with whomever you can to not be around them, though you might not have much power. Be ready to process it as an adult.

### Rewiring Your Brain

I haven’t finished the book yet so I’m not sure I have done much other than introspect a bit, but I think that’s all part of the process. If you want to change the way your brain works, especially a part you can’t control very easily, it’s going to take some *soul* searching.
