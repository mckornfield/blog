### A Thought Experiment

Imagine if tomorrow, we started something different. Instead of expressing numbers as debt, we told people what their cashflow would look like. This would be true for taxes, for loans, for everything you want to buy.

- If you buy this car, you will pay $500 per month her 60 months
- If you take out student loans, you will pay $460 over *20 years*

Maybe things would be just the same, but for some, it might be more clear how screwed you are getting. Instead of by scary piles of debt, we would be handed a more reasonable “cashflow” document, which should also be scary but also more useful to our day to day. Instead of a “40,000 dollar loan with interest over 30 years it would be $500 a month. This balance sheet would be something you could weigh against your income, and your supposed future higher earnings (opefully).

This might help us see the reality: that companies and the government want to saddle you with debt, to chip away at your wallet and point the arrows of cashflow their way.

- A car loan which comes with dealer junk like an “extended warranty”
- A student loan on a degree that might not get you [any more more than a high school diploma](https://universitybusiness.com/these-25-bachelors-degrees-earn-graduates-less-than-those-with-a-high-school-diploma/)
- A house loan that, should you sell it within a few years, will leave you [paying mostly interest upfront](https://smartasset.com/mortgage/home-loan-amortization), but over time if you pay the whole thing off, you still pay a *ton* in interest
- Credit card debt with atrociously high interest rates, that pile of debt grows faster than anything
- BNPL or by now pay later, which just tacks on fees and time to when you’re late paying the loan back

All of this to say: people/companies are very wiling to give you debt. *They’re mailing you cards and college applications*. So that extra cash will be pointed their way. What can we do to combat this?

### Debtizens or Citizens?

Every high debt them above has a low or no debt equivalent. If we went to reverse or at least slow the flowing of the cash flow rivers, we need to

- Rent small or buy small, keeping your place as small as possible to ensure you aren’t paying oodles a month for a big yard or extra bedroom
- Buy a used car, or avoid buying a car at all and (gasp) use public transit. Even Uber + buses end up being cheaper than a mid level car payment.
- Go to community college and transfer if you still want a degree with pedigree, or attend pubic instead of private lower fees overall
- If you can’t pay your CC every month, only use a debit card
- Don’t use BNPL!

### tl;dr

Debt can seem like a good idea in the moment, especially when you’re young, but remember Cashflows are all you have. Debt constrains your future lifestyle, to the point where you will be able to do very little besides earn and pay off your loans and their interest.

If you want to achieve surplus value you need:

- Rich parents
- A lot of squeezed pennies

The first is luck, the second is all in your hands.

### The greatest investment is you

After bashing student loan debt I want to zoom out and say: education or upskilling are the best things to pour your money into.

With one caveat: **think about the cashflow.**

If you want to be a doctor, lawyer etc. You might make decent money, but you will have 7-8 years worth of pre and post graduate education you need to pay for.

Consider that, if you don’t have half a million dollars via your parents or otherwise, you should do what you can (community college? public schools?) to reduce your debt load.

There are other ways to upskill as well, like unpaid internships, online courses, and just “doing the work!” These days there’s a massive shortage of [manual labor for more than one reason](https://inleo.io/@revisesociology/americas-worker-shortage-its-more-than-a-manufacturing-issue-ecd), so looking for where the demand is instead of where your passion is will most likely lead to more cashflow pointed your way.

### Entitlements and Social Security and Other Government Debt

It’s easy to see the US government’s complete mismanagement of funds, and think that it’s OK to take on enormous debt.

But the government isn’t a model for debt.

When the US gov’t hits a deficit (as it has every year for the last 20+), they just create more treasurys (i.e., create dollars and bonds). States, cities and us mere mortals don’t have his power.

Treasurys also represent a short term win, long term problem. Every deficit year, the UShas to spend more and more of its budget paying interest on debt.

If we go back to cashflows, that means more dollars flow from the US government to people who hold treasurys (rich people and foreign governments).

Other programs like SNAP or Social Security also represent cash conundrums. Social security, at its inception, had a very healthy ratio of young worker to retiree ratio. That ratio, 42 to 1 in the 40s, is now 3 to 1. Three people working for every person receiving.

Social security is a true cashflow game, taking money from young, productive people and handing it to old, non productive people.

It’s also a tax that is capped, meaning high earners aren’t hurt as much by it as middle income earner. But it’s a scheme where money is lifted from young earners paychecks and placed into old “earners” bank accounts.

It’s one thing to think that the money is “yours” and that you’ll receive it eventually, and another to realize that all money taken from you by force can be withheld from you by the same force.

Debt is a construct, just like social security and SNAP, but cashflows are real. Depending on a government that can’t manage to stay open, hasn’t formed a balanced budget in 20-years, and is incapable of investing in youth or workers is like depending on an abusive parent.

The only freedom is to distance yourself from reliance on such a parent. Make your own money and squirrel it away, so you don’t have to rely on whatever books Uncle Sam has cooked.
