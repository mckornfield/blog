### There’s More To Fear Than Fear Itself

If we don’t move, keep ourselves safe, find food and shelter, we die. This is true if we’re an “advanced” human or a “primitive” worm. The instinct that drives us can be positive, in the form of endorphins or dopamine, or it can be more basic, information passing through our [amygdala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amygdala), the seat of fear, anxiety and aggression.

It’s easy to see fear as a net negative in the human animal, something we must suppress and eliminate; drown it with drugs and therapy. But I think we do ourselves a disservice by not praising our good fears, and not differentiating them from our bad or useless fears.

## Good Fears

Let’s start with the good. The most obvious is what I lead into the article with: fears that keep you alive.

When you go off towards [the edge of a cliff](https://www.williambuist.com/2021/01/the-subtle-art-of-not-giving-a-fck-by-mark-manson/#:~:text=The%20final%20chapter,could%20relate%20to.), or plunge your head into the ocean, the part of your brain yelling at you “this will definitely kill you if you’re not careful” is the primary thing that keeps you alive. The survival aspect of fear is its greatest gift to us. Those who are unafraid adrenaline junkies only have to ignore fear one time that doesn’t go well.

### SMART Fears

But there are other fears that are good, I might even characterize them by the self improvement term [SMART](https://www.mindtools.com/a4wo118/smart-goals) fears. These are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. To use the survival fears as an example, let’s say you’re wavering on the edge of a cliff:

- Specific — Wow there is a really large cliff right in front of me
- Measurable — I can see with my eyes the cliff drop is at least a few hundred feet
- Achievable — If I step over the cliff, gravity will definitely do the rest
- Relevant — This cliff is right in front of me
- Time-Bound — This matters right now, but didn’t matter until I got to the cliff and won’t matter as soon as I step away

SMART fears are not just fears that keep us alive, but help us perform at our best or do the right thing. They have a clear outcome presented and a clear action to take.

- If I don’t study for this test, I’ll fail and have to repeat the class (so I’d better study!)
- If I don’t keep going to the gym, I’ll get out of shape and hate how I look (so get those gym shorts on!)
- If I don’t show up to work on time, my boss is going to think less of me (so get out of bed dang it!)

The list of SMART fears go on and on. I think all high achievers have an element of fear/anxiety that pushes them forward, as well as a desire to achieve and be the best that *pulls* them forward.

But I think fear is the bigger motivator because it taps into something more primal; fear’s links to survival mean that it’s built deeper into your brain, into your limbic system. Fear is both a motivator and a teacher; it strengthens memories and affects how we process the world.

In my life, *fear* has been the motivator for me when nothing else matters, when I don’t have a clear sense of what I’m striving for, I know what I want to strive *away from*.

I never wanted to be out of shape once I had been through middle school; my fear of my flabby self motivates my skinny self forward. I never thought of myself as a star student, but when I saw the disappointment from my teachers when I did poorly in their class; that was a much bigger motivator than any A or B. I saw people who spent so much time dealing with their drug habits that I knew that was a path I never wanted to walk down. Fear was (and is) my motivator to walk the right path.

That being said, all things can be abused, and that brings me to —

## Bad Fears

If I said SMART fears were good, then what are bad ones? I would characterize all these fears as “fears that cause you to freeze up” or a *fear paralysis*. Instead of being things that us drive to (or from) certain actions, these *bad fears* simply raise our cortisol levels and stop us from doing things that might actually benefit us.

There are a handful of categories for these fears:

- Existential fears (nuclear war, climate change)
- Irrational fears (heights, spiders, darkness)
- Societal fears (relationships, perception by peers)

### Existential Fears

You might argue that the existential fears have some SMART components to it, if you take certain actions based on your feelings about existential threats, but I’m describing the feelings of fear that something horrible will happen and you take no action to stop it. These are the fears characterized by nightmares like [Sarah Connor’s in Terminator 2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LqSMk2IzK2o) (although she was the mama of someone who *could* do something). They can be much smaller existential fears too, like that someone is coming to hurt you, though there’s no real evidence of it (*paranoia*).

### Irrational Fears

The irrational fears are a bit oddly named, since I think they’re linked in some way to the survival component I mentioned in the good fears section (fear of heights -> cliff metaphor, fear of spiders -> insects/spiders can be poisonous or venomous, fear of darkness -> scary things lurk in the dark). That being said, being afraid of heights to the point of simply freezing is the other side of the coin of a “good fear.” Fear should make you move, not freeze.

I was afraid of the dark for a long time. I’m still very jumpy, but I can navigate a dark space with a bit more courage than before (and I definitely keep moving). Spiders still freak me out… too many legs. (2 is good, 4 is fine, 6+ is bad). Heights are fun! WEeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeeee —

### Societal Fears

Anyway… back to the other fears. The fear that I felt affected me the most as I grew up was a societal fear. There were many relationships I avoided or didn’t pursue because of fear. And these were partly tied to a fear of not being good enough: of being not worth people’s time.

This sort of fear is a self fulfilling prophecy; by wallowing in it, you do become someone not worthy of a time investment, in the form of a relationship. Like the irrational fears I had, maturing was the only remedy to these fears.

I got the gumption to ask a girl out in person in sophomore year of college; 19 years of being too scared to ask a woman if she wanted to spend time with me haha… In some sense it might have been for the best (my wife says I probably missed out on a lot of heartache), but in another sense, I might have matured sooner if I’d faced my fears.

### How to Fight Our Bad Fears

What do we do about our bad fears? We do what every hero does, accept they’re scary and do *something*, instead of staying frozen like a rabbit.

Last anecdote on bad fears; my Aunt and I were doing dishes when a spider poked out of a glass (we’re both afraid of spiders). I said “OK I’m going to conquer my fear,” proceeded to put the glass in the sink, spray the spider with water (which launched it out at us), then screamed, did the little “get off me spider” dance, and then laugh.

If you can’t fight your fears with courage, laughter is a good next choice.

### Summary

Fight the bad fears by pushing forward and doing *something*. Let the good ones push you to keep moving forward and being a better you.

We have nothing to fear but letting the bad fears consume us, and ignoring the good fears.
