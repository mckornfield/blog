## Does Going to a Good American College Matter?

Yes… because they’re hard to get into.

Low acceptance rates and ultra high tuition means your degree has weight.

If you want a four word answer to why you’re deserving access to the upper echelons of society, it’s: “I went to Harvard/Princeton/etc.”

Just look at the ranks of [Supreme Court Justices](https://madison365.com/of-the-116-supreme-court-justices-in-us-history-all-but-8-have-been-white-men/) or [Presidents of the US](https://www.uopeople.edu/blog/where-did-most-u-s-presidents-attend-college/#:~:text=Of%20the%2044%20men%20who,them%20graduated%20from%20college%20overall.).

College admissions does the selection job for employers, who then hook themselves up to elite universities through career fairs and alumni networks. You’re guaranteed success if you pick a decent major at one of these institutions; these degrees are worth around [$30,000 initially, and $60,000 later](https://www.usnews.com/education/best-colleges/articles/how-much-is-an-ivy-league-degree-worth#:~:text=Early%2Dcareer%20%28which%20PayScale%20defines,20%20years%20experience%29%20median%20pay.), in terms of increased wages.

So what does it take to get in? I can only offer you —

## My Experience, And What Could Have Gone Better

I got into Georgetown when applying to schools in 2011. It was by far the best school I got into, and it is for sure a decent school. At the time it had an 18% acceptance rate. I got in I believe because I had decent grades, an okay SAT score, and was pretty well rounded, and I applied to the Physics program, which was pretty small.

At the time I was sad because I didn’t get into any better schools; I thought it was because of a personal failing, and that was in part true.

In retrospect, it’s a matter of where effort was placed, and knowledge about what things *matter*. I’ll try to summarize what I’ve learned about getting into college from seeing other’s success.

## “Learn How to Package Yourself”

I had an interview with a Harvard alum, who looked over my credentials when I had applied to the school. I had an interest in languages, and had taken Chinese and Spanish classes. I had participated in one writing contest and one, but that was it for writing. I played an instrument, but not that well. Decent grades. Decent SATs. Yawn.

After talking through my credentials, he compared me to a woman who had graduated the year before and got into Harvard. We were both on the literary magazine, but she had something I didn’t.

He talked about her exceptional ballet skills and writing talent. He talked about how amazingly smart she was (valedictorian/salutatorian).

And then he hit me with something I won’t forget:

“You need to learn how to package yourself.” He explained about how when he was in school, he did these math/science competitions. That he could come across as a really intelligent kid because he had immersed himself in learning inside AND outside of school. He’d found a way to package himself.

I knew that I definitely wasn’t getting in just based on talking with him, but also knew I’d be pretty doomed with other college interviews. I’d set myself on a path to be decent at a set of random, unrelated things, but not *exceptional* at any of them.

Nothing that would garner favor at the hands of the admissions department.

But these days I think I know what I could have done better, and what you can do for yourself or anyone you know who’s trying to get into these colleges, including kids or younger relatives.

## What Does it Mean to Package Yourself?

There’s a simple recipe for being exceptional, even as a 17 year old looking to fight your way into an elite university. It’s basically this:

1. Do More Things
2. Do Fewer Things
3. Do Something Exceptional

Let’s break these down a bit

### **Do More Things**

Kids who get into elite universities don’t just show up to school and get good grades. They’re also star athletes, or exceptionally good at another skill, like a musical instrument or writing.

For me as a kid, this would have probably meant leaning into writing or computer programming; I had opportunities in both of these places, and a bit of skill when it came to writing, but stayed away from them (and what do you know, I ended up fighting my way back to them in my professional life, womp womp).

Do More Things also means do more when it comes to preparing for exams/ getting good grades in school itself. I took a class to study for the SATs, and wouldn’t you know, I got better at taking it.

These tests aren’t about raw intelligence, they’re really about practice and work. You aren’t getting into these elite universities without being the top of your class. All the valedictorians and salutatorians from my school got into Ivy League colleges, so there’s a pretty straightforward answer.

Kids who do more invest their time inside and outside of school wisely, so that that on an application form, they stand out.

The other side of doing *more* things is to —

### **Do Fewer Things**

I had all of these middling skills, like playing the saxophone, some language skills, but nothing that really helped me stand out. These middling skills should all have been relegated to the trash heap, or at least the hobby heap.

If only I’d gotten the packaging talk freshman year instead of senior year :’(

This meant dumping the saxophone when there were 10+ other saxophones in band, half of which were better than me. (I ended up playing the same part as the French Horns, and I’ll never forget the time where the band teacher said we were too loud, so we just stopped playing, and he said “that’s perfect!”)

This also would have meant dumping a Chinese course I took that didn’t really teach me much. Maybe even dumping Spanish. (There are, as it turns out, a LOT of bilingual Spanish speakers in the US, around 40+ million).

Doing Fewer Things also means doing fewer things that aren’t going to show up on a college application (for me that meant not playing nearly as many video games, especially World of Warcraft).

Ideally I would have married my desire for competition and play with something more constructive like sports, or even the math team!

But I didn’t push myself to do more like I should have. As an adult I found running is my best solace, but as a kid I could barely run a mile. Sigh.

I don’t look back fondly at all the times I sat on the computer and played games, or poorly played my part on the saxophone. It was just a waste; something I should have quit not because it was hard, but because it was *easy* and not exceptional.

Future me has had to bear the consequences of past me wasting my time. All I can do is warn others not to do the same, because you’ll have to do extra learning you could have done when you were, you know, in school…

In writing they use the term “[kill your darlings](https://www.reddit.com/r/writing/comments/15kinpw/kill_your_darlings/),” for excising the parts of writing you’re attached to, but which actually suck.

As a kid becoming an adult, and one that wants to get into an elite university, you have to excise the parts of yourself that aren’t going to make a difference in your college admission, or in your life. All for the sake of packaging yourself!

I want to emphasize this point because I think the hard part of getting into a good school (or hell, having any sort of success) is not the things you say yes to, but the things you say **no** to.

Saying *no* is what helps make you exceptional, and speaking of which —

### Do Something Exceptional

This is the cheat code to getting into these colleges, if there is one. And the easiest way to help package yourself.

One of the girls in my high school year got into Yale I believe through this sort of mechanism; I don’t think she had immeasurably more talent or smarts than me (not one of the salutatorians), but she had *hosted an art show*… how was I supposed to compete with that?

A cornerstone of a good college application is your essay. You’re going to write about something you did or experienced. What better way to write a memorable essay than to have an exceptional thing you did, which you can package yourself around.

Let’s just run a hypothetical for how it could have gone differently for me.

[My brother has a pretty debilitating mental disorder](https://mckornfield.medium.com/my-brother-is-a-silent-mirror-of-me-72f37a16ba8f), being nonverbal and Autistic PDD-NOS (i.e., they have no freaking clue what he actually has). My mom said at one point to me: “you could help create a learning app that Mariana (his therapist) could work with you on.” At the time making an app seemed scary (gasp, writing code!), so I refused.

But if I had written the app, what a way to package myself.

I could write about how I both cared about my brother and used my “brain power” to try and help him. And maybe it would have been performative (amazing app quality, 5 stars, you 16 year old programmer!), but most of this stuff is; I was still a kid.

Wouldn’t have meant it wasn’t exceptional.

Instead, I had nothing exceptional to write about (my essay was about being someone who cares about others; it was no better than a Hallmark card). And I’m sure most higher ed admissions people immediately put me in the “no thank you” pile.

So how do kids do something exceptional? I think there’s one simple answer:

### Exceptional Kids Lean on the Adults in Their Life

Kids don’t have access to unlimited resources and connections, unless they’re ultra wealthy or royalty.

Instead, the best resources they have are their parents and any other adults (yay good teachers) who are willing to help them out.

The girl who put on the art show? I think there’s a 100% chance that she got help from an adult in her life. And good on her. It set her on a path to being very successful.

Part of me not writing the app was being unwilling to seek and lean on someone who would have helped me. My school had a computer science class and teachers, I just shied away from it because it was hard (do more things), and I had other things I was already doing (do fewer things).

But because I refused to see the best way to package myself, I didn’t lean on those adults and didn’t become an exceptional kid. I was perfectly ordinary. In fact, I was probably pretty lucky to get into Georgetown.

## Summary

If you’re looking to get into an American College with a low acceptance rate (most of these elite places accept less than 5% of applicants), you’re going to want to package yourself in a way that makes you stand out and above the 1000s of other applications, that means:

1. **Do More Things** Inside and Outside School to Stand Out
2. **Do Fewer Things** That *Don’t* Make You Stand Out
3. **Do Something Exceptional**, Probably with the Help of Adults in Your Life

Good luck!
