And why you should probably turn it off too

![Photo by John M. Smit on Unsplash](/img/turning-off-copilot-was-a-good-idea/0_9-X7pTw-ORtjTsOL.jpg)

*Photo by [John M. Smit](https://unsplash.com/@john_smit?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)*

I experimented with using GitHub Copilot for a bit to (help) write my code; it supercharged my productivity, but something felt off about it. If you don’t know what GitHub Copilot is, it’s a AI tool produced by GitHub that can write/suggest code for you as you’re writing it.

I was testing out an interview question for work ([which Copilot could solve without issue](/posts/does-github-copilot-mean-we-can-stop-doing-live-coding-inter/)) so I decided to turn it off… And I haven’t turned it back on.

I feel like, in some sense, I’m less productive with it off, but I also feel like moving faster while not knowing what’s happening isn’t all that helpful.

I believe the tool has a place (boilerplate?), but that I want to be the one pressing the keys. Let me explain.

### Watch a Kid Learn

I have a 3 year old daughter. I watched her learn to crawl, to walk, and to use the potty. She’s still in the process of learning to talk, but getting better every day.

What watching her learn has drilled into me multiple times is: she doesn’t really learn much by watching me do it.

That is: me *showing* her how to do something doesn’t directly translate to her *being able* to do it. She has to push her own body through the motions, and fail over and over before getting it right.

I can try and soften some landings, or try and move her limbs for her to guide her properly, but the best thing I can do is be a set of training wheels. She has to do the moving herself.

Training wheels are a great analogy for any good learning tool: you still perform the motion, but something can come and keep you from falling over and crashing hard.

Kids (and adults) learn by doing. Not by watching. Another example —

### Watching Someone Else Play a Video Game

A common passtime amongst gamers is to watch another gamer play. It doesn’t mean you’ll mechanically be able to beat any of the content that you’re watching onscreen, but it does give you at least a rough outline of what can happen and how to be prepared. Like spark notes, but for games.

But I’ll repeat what I said before: you can’t beat a game just because you watch someone else do it, unless the game is a puzzle game and you have a great memory, you’re going to need to go through the motions yourself.

That being said, watching someone play a game can *prepare* you.

Back when I played Dark Souls (and got my butt kicked by it), I watched someone play through the first few levels. That was in a sense *cheating* (I knew which creatures were going to pop out and spook me), but it was also like a set of training wheels: I had enough knowledge to fumble my way through as I was learning the controls.

But, I still had to move the controller by myself to make progress.

Now, back to programming —

### DHH: Copilot as a Google replacement, not a coding replacement

One of the things that inspired me to keep Copilot off was this interview with [DHH](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mTa2d3OLXhg&feature=youtu.be), the guy who invented [Ruby on Rails](https://dhh.dk/) among other things.

When asked about AI assisted coding in the interview, he said it’s a great replacement for StackOverflow and Google, but that he likes the act of typing the words.

That resonates with me; as a reference tool, these AI systems can not just produce code but *explain* it, and pretty quickly.

Instead of plopping 50 lines of code into the editor and dusting off its hands, Copilot instead can suggest some code and tell me why it works. Then I can either blindly copy paste it or (hopefully) type out my version of things; it can be my teacher and my guide, but not my personal code typer, because —

### Typing Matters

I like writing on a keyboard. It’s the best way I can take my thoughts and lay them out in a proper fashion (I tend to blabber when I speak). I can go back and edit things and fine tune them as well. My brain is not nearly as coherent as an edited piece of writing is.

When it comes to programming, there’s generally more firepower at your disposal. There are tools like ViM, which if you’ve never heard of it is a high powered text editor that can be [painfully confusing if it’s your first time using it](https://stackoverflow.com/a/11828573/10061484).

Integrated Development Environments (IDE)s offer even more , beyond simply moving around files and typing in characters. There are crazy cool shortcuts and refactoring tools.

All that being said, these tools are like hammers and screwdrivers. *You* still have to move your arm to get them to work, but they can help you realize the vision of what you’re trying to create with a lot fewer keystrokes.

GitHub Copilot is *not* like these tools; instead producing code that might be *exactly* what you want or close enough, but also, not necessarily teaching you about coding while it does it.

It’s like a black box you throw a piece of wood in, and a table comes out the other side.

Typing matters because *doing things* matter. Just thinking or talking about them is insufficient (boo ivory tower). My kid learns by moving her own legs, people learn to overcome games by playing them, and I learn to program by typing and trying things out.

The way I interface with a computer is clicking or typing, and typing is faster. So I will continue to type, and if I have questions, I’ll type out a question to one of the tools that can help me, and read the answer.

Typing is how I can both express myself AND learn by doing.

### Summary

When I type, I use my brain. When I use Copilot, I don’t. I just hit tab and pray. 🙏🙏

I like using my brain, it keeps the thing working. 🧠🧠

If I need to learn something, I can use Copilot, or Google, or StackOverflow or (gasp) actually documentation. 📚📚

I’ll keep Copilot off so that I can still swing the metaphorical hammer. But it’s a good teacher. 👩‍🏫🧑‍🏫
