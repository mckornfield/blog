### My Background

I went into school to study physics, mostly because I wanted to study engineering but went to a school (Georgetown) that didn’t offer any sort of engineering majors. It *did* offer a computer science major, but after taking an introductory class, I was pretty put off the subject. I took the CS101 course and audited the CS102 course (I basically turned in all the projects and sat for the final). I also took a computational physics class and didn’t get much of anything out of it.

It came down to the fact that, at least in my opinion, teaching software development is really different from teaching computer science.

Wanting something outside of a career in physics/ academia, I turned to technology consulting, thanks to a fellow physics student who’d already graduated. When I started in technology consulting, I found a huge variety of players at different level of technical skillset. People in the same role (consultant) could be computer science majors, lawyers, or just a dopey physics major like myself. I’d spent more time hand-writing things out in school than typing away on the computer, but the computer now became my best bud. We went through a lot together that first year.

After being given the opportunity to move to software development after the first year (as a “product engineer”, a role in between software engineer and consultant), I really started to see an even wider variety of technical skillsets, as I met some software architects, database experts, security experts, and all sorts of folks that ran the gamut in the software world.

### Software/IT as an Essential Industry

It wasn’t too long ago that software was a woman’s job. The first generation of computer programmers, programming enormous, vacuum tube mainframes, were largely female. Hardware was a “man’s job.” At that time, the uses for a computer were purely what its name suggests, computing things that a generation before had been done with complex mechanical devices, and a generation before that had been done by people, by hand (also mostly by women).

But the transistor, and the kings of software (Gates and Jobs) brought in a new era, where the computer became, as I found when I started working, *the cornerstone* of many, if not all industries.

- Banks use computers for high frequency trading and traditional transactions.
- Stores use computers as PoS machines, and to sell merchandise online.
- Cars are some of the most advanced computers for sale these days, with vision systems and automatic parking/lane keeping
- If you’re a software company… your whole business model is built on the fact that other people/businesses own and use computers that you hope to also run or use your software

No industry has been left untouched by the personal computer revolution. With that comes the fact that no *person* is untouched. If you want to be ultra successful, you’ll need to master the technologies of our time, or build the new ones. If you discount Saudi Aramco (which, like every multi billion dollar energy company, is built on oil), the [top 5 market cap companies](https://companiesmarketcap.com/) are all technology companies (Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, NVIDIA).

These companies provide tools, products and services that the planet has become reliant on to run their economies. The top 3 companies basically represent all major commercial and personal operating systems (sorry Linux, barely anyone uses you for a personal machine) and all mobile operation systems (well there are only 2 so…).

### Low Code, No Code, AI Coding (oh my)

So back to my story of slowly worming my way into the technology industry. I joined at a time when certain systems were fairly mature (public Cloud) and as some were just starting to become useful (Neural Network AI). And the first company I joined was touted as a “low code” platform, and certain pieces of our product were also touted as “no code.”

Plenty of sites and tools offer no code options, like Weebly or Squarespace, so you can make a website without any JS knowledge. Excel probably touts itself as a low code offering, though now it [lets you write Python there](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/t5/excel-blog/announcing-python-in-excel-combining-the-power-of-python-and-the/ba-p/3893439).

Github Copilot and ChatGPT are capable of writing code themselves. So pretty soon we won’t need software developers, now will we? We’ll just ask an AI to write the code and throw together the whole site… sounds pretty straightforward, yeah?

### Why These Tools Don’t Change the Calculus

*Low/No Code* — The essential problem with a low or no code system, like the one I used to work with and for, is that they are still *very technical*. Making decisions about how to design things, how to scale, how to build securely, all mapped pretty well to programming concepts that I later learned.

Just because you’re not writing “code”, you’re still telling a computer to perform a very specific set of instructions. And those specific instructions can cause errors, or run into scaling issues, or hit some weird integration hurdle that means you can’t get two systems to properly talk to each other.

The other thing about low/no code systems is that they can leave you as the user very *powerless* if the tool doesn’t support it. This leaves you with two categories of options

1. Live with it
2. Add some code to do what you want, via a plugin, or another service, or advocating for the platform to prioritize the work

The truth about these systems is that they *aren’t* low/no code, they’re just code that someone else writes, that you use through an abstraction layer. You could argue using the GUI of an iPhone or a desktop computer is low/no code, but there’s millions of lines of code that power what you’re using.

It’s like being vegetarian, but eating crops that are grown on land where rabbits/deer are killed for trying to eat the lettuce… yeah you’re not eating meat, but you still rely on animal death to consume…

*AI Coding —*I’d say this has a strong correlation with other fields we worry will be replaced with AI, like writing, or being a lawyer. If you break AI coding down, you’ll essentially have 3 parts:

1. The prompt (please make me a website about kittens)
2. The response (here is a Flask app that has kitten information in it)
3. You editing and “deploying” that component

That third step is way more work than steps 1 and 2. You might ask the AI coding utility HOW you would do certain things, but they’re just text generation tools; we haven’t yet gotten to the point where AI actually *control* systems, and before we get there, we’ll have to think long and hard about whether or not it’s worth it.

AI don’t have value systems like we do; they build neural networks based on ways that we “teach” them, through different reinforcement or adversarial learning systems. The values that we’d have to give AI (don’t let my website be taken over by attackers) are much harder to get right than we think.

Conversely, I think the AI tools have the power to make people way more productive. When using GitHub Copilot, I found its suggestions weren’t perfect, but way better than the typical flipping back and forth between docs and example code.

### What Lies Under the Hood

I consider myself among the Tech/IT folk, but who knows how long I’ll be among them; I might well be supplanted in a generation or two by the AI tools I’m lambasting. But I figure I’ll end this discussion with a metaphor that might be relevant.

Imagine you live in a world where everyone is highly dependent on their cars to get around, but if they have trouble/break down, the only people who will help you charge *crazy* amounts of money. In that world, you have two choices:

1. Make the car mechanics super rich by giving them all your money
2. Opening the hood and figuring it out on your own

And as the world becomes so strongly dominated by software, being able to lift the hood/bonnet and sort through what’s going on will not only be a requirement, it’ll be a survival skill. A way to not just give technical people all your money for doing who knows what. And no amount of tooling is going to help you; you need to know a bit about how things actually work.
