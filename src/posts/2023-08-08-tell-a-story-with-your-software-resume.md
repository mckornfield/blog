Recently, I sat down with a young woman who wanted some advice on her resume. While it was a good exercise, I think some things came out of talking with her that I think everyone can benefit from if they’re trying to touch up their resume and get back out there.

### Emphasize things you’ve accomplished and their impact

> Star light, star bright, first star I see tonight.

The STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) methodology, if you haven’t heard of it, is a tried and true way to explain your history. It’s the interviewer’s/interviewee’s template for presenting things you’ve done in a consumable format and one that can be translated to the current job.

### **Don’t do this**

Consider bullets in a resume that don’t follow the STAR format:

- Set up an AKS cluster with HITRUST certification
- Reduced production deployment times by five minutes

These bullets, while they have jargon-y words, tell no story at all. It’s just the “A” of the “STAR” format. You want your resume bullets to tell a story.

### **Do this**

Consider the following bullets a mirror of the above:

- Rolled out a production-grade HITRUST AKS Cluster within a month to meet multiple government contract deadlines
- Reduced a 10-minute production deployment time by five minutes, drastically improving rollback capabilities

While a bit meatier, they give context (the Situation) and present the Result, meeting deadlines or improving rollback capabilities.

You want the questions that people ask you about your resume to be more on the HOW side of things, so they can get details about how you work and what your strengths are, but the WHY should be self-evident from the resume itself. Each bullet should tell a story or be part of a larger story.

### Put what you know as a footnote

> I know Kung Fu.

> — Neo (Keanu Reeves), the Matrix

Unless you are Neo and have indeed just learned Kung Fu via neural upload, you shouldn’t spend much time going over the sorts of tools and technologies you’ve learned. Even Neo only spends four words on it!

Instead, put those in a skills section on its own, so people can check boxes when scanning your resume.

An interviewer, though, probably doesn’t care much about *what* you know and *why* you know those things. They care about what you use them for.

### **Don’t do this**

Stuff your resume with various tools and technologies to make yourself sound like the greatest software person ever.

### **Do this**

- Put things like Python, certifications, etc., in a skills box
- Show how you used those tools to accomplish tasks that make a difference in your life or in roles you’ve had

### How to package “changing roles”

The person whose resume I was reviewing had a lot of projects listed and then work experience, but in a different role. They were still in the software field but had worked as a project manager, and after getting their master’s degree, this person would change to a software engineer.

I know this story well, considering my degree was in Physics, and I ended up in the software field. I have much growing to do as an engineer in many ways, but I have the storytelling right these days.

### **Don’t do this**

What the person whose resume I reviewed did was downplay her past experience.

There are a handful of problems with this, including the following:

1. It appears as a gap in terms of work, etc.
2. It makes it seem like you sprouted out of the Earth after you got your degree

Any work you can show is better than none, but your best bet instead will be…

### **Do this**

Integrate who you were with who you are and who you will be.

Make it a story.

For the person whose resume I was reviewing, this means expanding their work experience section to show how they’d worked with developers and grew interested in the software side — enough to want to make a career change. She might do this with an opening statement on her resume as well. Something like

“Former project manager now trained as a software engineer. Someone who can write code but also understand requirements and what it takes to run a successful software project.”

For the story I tell about myself, I say that I like solving problems and clarifying things. The line from physics to consulting to software development has that common thread of “explanatory problem solver.”

Part of what makes good storytelling is having a unique story, and your background will most likely be unique, whether that’s interests you had outside work or the winding path that took you to your current job. If you feel like the path is awkward or won’t come across in the interview well, you don’t need to lie or hide anything. You need to think about how you can tell a compelling story.

## **Summary**

Make your resume tell stories.

Stories about the things you accomplished, about the person you are and will be if you get the job, and don’t forget to make your skills a footnote or a supplement, not the main focus, of your resume.
