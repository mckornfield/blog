The Great American Pastime, Explained

If you’re about to head to a game for the first time or watch one on TV, this might help as a reference or something to help understand what you see on the screen.

*Photo by [Joshua Peacock](https://unsplash.com/@jcpeacock?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)*

Baseball is mechanically a lot more complicated than American Football, mostly because it’s not 11 guys trying to stop another 11 guys from moving 10 yards with a ball.

Baseball, like Cricket, is an asymmetric game, where defense and offense are pretty different. But to try and use the analogy my wife had for American Football:

“Each team has 9 (or more if still tied) attempts to hit a ball so they can round 3 bases and reach home plate. Each attempt comes with 3 **outs**.”

The goal of baseball is to have more **runs** than the opposing team after a set of **innings** is completed (turns playing offense or defense).

A run is scored one of two ways:

1. A **batters** hits the baseball thrown by the **pitcher**, and no one is able to get them **out** as they make their way around the bases (we’ll get to what that means)
2. A player hits a **home run** (into the stands, between two large posts in the **outfield**), giving themselves and anyone on base a run

You’ll see a lot of numbers on the scoreboard and they are all relevant to how the game works:

- The numbers 1 through 9+ for each team represent the **innings**, or attempts to **bat** (offense) versus **field** the ball (defense). If the game is tied after 9 innings, major league baseball has **extra innings**
- Each part of the inning (**top of the inning** and **bottom of the inning**) has a number, the number of **runs** scored that inning

The very end of each row has:

- R (the total number of **runs**),
- H (the number of **hits**, i.e., times a player hit the ball and safely made it to a base), and
- E (the number of **errors**, i.e., dropped balls, bad throws, etc.)

Each inning has stats that constantly reset, these are the number of **outs** and the **count.**

The **count** is sometimes just presented as two numbers, e.g. 3–2 or 0–0. It describes the state of the batter.

- The first number is the number of **balls**, or throws that the player did not swing at, and were not valid throws (i.e. outside of the **strike zone,** or basically a box as tall the player’s torso and as wide as home plate).
- The second number is the number of **strikes**, or throws that the player either swung at and missed, or that they didn’t swing at but were valid throws, OR were **fouls**

**Fouls** are illegal hits (i.e. hits that go into the stands, but are not home runs, or hits that are outside of the first and third baselines). A foul counts as a strike for the first two, but cannot be the third strike, which is why you might see the count stay still as the batter knocks balls in weird directions.

When a player receives 4 balls, they **walk** (i.e. they take first base, and progress another runner forward if a different runner were on first base).

When a player receives 3 strikes (remembering that fouls can’t be the third), they **strikeout**, meaning they are **out**, and if that is the 3rd out, the inning ends.

The **Umpire** (or Referee) behind home plate will point to call **strikes**, but will simply stand up if a **ball** is thrown.

If a player is hit with the ball, it’s an automatic **walk**, regardless of the count.

A few other ways players can get **out**:

- If the runner is **tagged** by a fielder with the ball in their glove (either while the runner is trying to steal a base, or off base), the player is **out**.
- If a fielder has the ball in his glove and steps on the base during a **force out** (i.e. the runner has nowhere else he can run back to). This usually happens when the fielder is thrown the ball after it hits the ground (**groundout)**
- If a fielder catches the ball in mid air, the player is out (**flyout**)

When a player is running/sliding into a base while a player attempts to tag/ force out the player:

- If the **Umpire** crosses their arms, they are **safe**.
- If the **Umpire** does a one armed pumping motion, then the player is **out**.

That should cover 90% of the game. [There’s other stats and numbers that are cool](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/standard-stats), but just be aware they represent how likely a player is to either hit the ball or simply get on base (i.e. including walks and getting hit).

Recently, a pitch clock was added in Major League Baseball to help keep the game moving (the pitcher AND the batter can be penalized for not being ready by one ball or one strike respectively), but the pace of the game is still very explosive/tension based.

As more runners are on base, the chances of scoring increase, putting pressure on the pitcher and fielders to not let any more runners hit.

*This is the time to pay attention.* Most games have a base indicator, showing which bases are occupied. The more bases (and more cheering if it’s the home team), the more you should pay attention.

For the rest of the game you can just chat with folks or go get an overpriced beer/ hot dog.

Thanks for reading, hopefully this helps you understand the games!
