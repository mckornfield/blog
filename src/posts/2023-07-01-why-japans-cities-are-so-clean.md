Some recent takeaways from my visit to Tokyo and Osaka

![Shinagawa Station in Toyko, with no trash in sight](/img/why-japans-cities-are-so-clean/0_EWm7esg1nsDz0zAS.jpg)

*Shinagawa Station in Toyko, with no trash in sight*

I recently visited Japan for a week and found the cities so incredibly clean, commensurate with some of the cities I’d visited in Europe, but supporting much larger population bases.

Stockholm was one of the cleanest cities I’d visited, but with a population of just under 1 million, it doesn’t compare with another city I visited that was immaculate, Tokyo, a city with 14 times as many people.

Kyoto, the ninth largest city in Japan, and Osaka, the third largest city, were also very clean. Here are the reasons I think these cities are so clean:

- The homeless population is very small (percentage-wise)
- There are no public trashcans
- There are tons of public restrooms
- Respectful people

And why does cleanliness matter? Beyond promoting healthiness, it makes the city a place worth going to. All cities should strive to be as clean as the cities in Japan.

### Small Homeless Populations

There are some more nuanced reasons that Japan’s homeless population is small (for example, not very many refugees compared with countries like Germany), but I’ll focus on the big picture here.

When I was in Osaka, I saw the most homeless people that I’d seen in Japan, but this was one every kilometer or so. Compare that with the city I live right near (Portland) and it’s pretty shocking how few homeless there are in a city that’s roughly 4 times the size of Portland.

- Osaka: 2.6 million inhabitants, ~900 homeless (0.035%)
- Portland: 600 thousand inhabitants, 1604 homeless (0.267%)

I think this is probably the single greatest contributor to a lack of trash in the cities. It’s easy to blame folks who get food wrappers and just throw them on the streets (and I’ll get to them), but the largest concentration of trash and waste in cities is around homeless camps.

![Homeless camps on the side of the freeway in Portland OR](/img/why-japans-cities-are-so-clean/1_LSNKMw92qjS9c_ss3JN7nQ.jpeg.jpg)

*Homeless camps on the side of the freeway in Portland OR*

Bringing the focus back to Japan, part of why homeless people aren’t as widespread is it is [illegal to beg](https://tomorrow.city/a/homelessness-in-japan), and I bet it’s enforced. While I was there I saw a police officer ask someone to move from a seated position on the floor of the train platform. I don’t think I’ve ever seen that happen in the US.

It’s easy to blame externalities like housing prices or drug addiction, but let’s attack each of those in turn.

At [$8000 per square meter](https://www.numbeo.com/cost-of-living/city_price_rankings?itemId=100), Tokyo is significantly more expensive than Portland ($6500 per square meter). Both Tokyo and Osaka have much fewer homeless people as a percentage of the population than the cheaper Portland (for Tokyo, with ~600 homeless and 14 million people, it’s 0.004% of the population).

Part of Japan’s success against drug use is cultural, considering they are [pretty strongly against recreational drug use](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illegal_drug_trade_in_Japan#:~:text=Marijuana%20trade%20can%20happen%20in,to%20their%20reputations%20and%20careers.). The fact that Oregon has [decriminalized drugs and botched the treatment of addicts](https://apnews.com/article/health-oregon-government-and-politics-drug-addiction-05269fb778d33e0355db17bb8539c34c) only adds fire to the problem of homelessness, treating people’s addictions as a non criminal, voluntarily fixable behavior.

To summarize: a larger homeless presence produces more trash on the street. Reducing homelessness is one of the main ways Japan keeps its cities so immaculate.

### Almost No Public Trashcans

This floored me for a while, but then it made perfect sense. I bought a bottle from one of their many fancy street vending machines, and when I was walking around, I noticed there really weren’t any trashcans anywhere. The only trashcans I ever saw were near buildings/ vending machines themselves.

![One of the ubiquitous Japanese vending machines](/img/why-japans-cities-are-so-clean/0_HaMevF35YQRWSci_.jpg)

*One of the ubiquitous Japanese vending machines*

You’ll notice the trash cans above are for recycling only. I was so confused, what was I supposed to do with my trash?

And that’s when I started to realize the genius of the system.

In Portland, as in many other cities I’ve visited in the US, there are public trashcans on almost every street corner. But these are also nasty spots, where the trashcans become overloaded, or people looking for recycling cans empty all the trash onto the street just to get $1’s worth of soda cans. Dumpsters are no better, here’s an example from where I used to live near DC

![Trash, trash everywhere (a dumpster outside DC in northern VA)](/img/why-japans-cities-are-so-clean/0_Rh2WQyaE0weAo1-m.jpg)

*Trash, trash everywhere (a dumpster outside DC in northern VA)*

So if public trashcans actually make cities *dirtier* around the cans, then wouldn’t they be worse off if you remove the trashcans? Or still at least the same?

I think the answer, from the Japanese, is an emphatic **no** (or いいえ). When you don’t have the option to throw trash into overflowing cans, or dig through the trash, you options are to either:

- Throw trash on the street (gross)
- Take your trash back with you to where you live or where you’re staying

You end up being a “conscious trash user”. There aren’t opportunities for you to mess up and accidentally land trash on the street, because the only trash cans are ones that exist where you live.

Even when I bought street food, I had to hand back my trash to the vendor, because there were no cans to place it in. By removing the ability for you to just throw your trash in randomly placed cans, Japanese move the responsibility onto the trash holders, and not the city, for maintaining their cleanliness.

I do really think this is genius, and wonder if maybe American cities would benefit from eliminating trashcans.

On the flipside, while there were no public trashcans, there were —

### Lots of Public Restrooms

![A set of bathrooms, in Shin Osaka Station](/img/why-japans-cities-are-so-clean/1_U3DnW5SAQHu6nKfgkrPwWQ.jpeg.jpg)

*A set of bathrooms, in Shin Osaka Station*

I was floored. Every train station, every subway station, and many easily accessible malls all had public restrooms. They were also very clean, and many of them didn’t have trashcans either (air dryers only).

A public restroom is a disaster in many cities like Portland. When I’ve had to (unfortunately) use one of them, of which there are extremely few, there were always clothes or other strange things left in the bathroom, by people who were using these places as their personal bathrooms. This is probably the main reason why the US has such a dearth of public restrooms, so addressing homelessness would probably be a prerequisite.

But without fail, at any station, one could find a well taken care of restroom. Why are these important to cleanliness? Because many of the nastiest things that could happen on the street (human waste, used wipes, etc.) are unnecessary if bathroom access is a given.

For the small homeless population that *does* exist (given that there will always be *some* homeless people), there’s no worry about them having to go to the bathroom in the street, or someone stumbling home from the bar at 1AM who needs to pee.

Public bathrooms are also places where people can clean themselves off, so the benefit extends beyond just waste (i.e. people have more opportunities to was their hands).

The personal hygiene aspect I think brings me to my last point.

### Cultural Emphasis on Respect

Japanese people, in my brief interaction with them, were almost always respectful. This meant waiting in line, saying excuse me (すみません!), and as mentioned in the section on trash, being conscious about what you did with your trash.

I noticed the Japanese people were always very careful with the packaging/bottles that they used, so that they were put away instead of lazily held in their hands while strolling around.

While this is an outsider’s view, I believe that this concept of respect also extended to the environment people lived in, so that they wouldn’t just throw trash on the ground.

Based on all the miles I walked around Tokyo and Osaka, I think the Japanese model the clean urbanite, at least by looking down at their clean, empty streets.

### In Summary

With Japan’s small homeless population, lack of trashcans, but proliferation of bathrooms, and a respectful populace, its cities are clean as can be, which makes them a joy to experience.
