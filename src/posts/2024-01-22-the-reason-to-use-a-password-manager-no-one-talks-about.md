### Why a Password Manager Again?

Passwords are a necessary evil of the internet… hopefully we’ll all be using [Passkeys](https://justpass.medium.com/passkeys-better-authentication-for-the-internet-3ee342550c0e) for things here soon, but until then, we’ll have to rely on usernames and passwords.

If you want to make your accounts as hack-proof as possible, using long, unique and hard to guess passwords is the way to go for password based accounts.

Doing so without a password manager is a difficult exercise. With the password manager, you can simply generate a long string of characters that fit the constraints of the site and store it right away, so you can easily login across devices.

But I think there’s a stronger reason to opt for a password manager, namely: contingencies.

### Bad Things Happen to All People

We will all die at some point, but our accounts don’t disappear with us. We could even be incapacitated before dying, but our accounts will be inaccessible, especially if we’ve used a password manager with a good master password and multifactor authentication.

The fact that it could take months or longer to access accounts of a loved one who’s been incapacitated or passed can be a real burden for people, especially if they need access to money to pay their bills or settle accounts.

Fortunately, all password managers that I know of offer a mechanism to share individual passwords, and all that I’m aware of allow you to share your whole account, e.g., Emergency Access for [LastPass](https://www.lastpass.com/features/emergency-access#:~:text=Log%20in%20to%20your%20LastPass,time%2C%20you%20are%20granted%20access.https://www.lastpass.com/features/emergency-access#:~:text=Log%20in%20to%20your%20LastPass,time%2C%20you%20are%20granted%20access.) or [Keeper](https://docs.keeper.io/user-guides/emergency-access) in the event of an emergency.

So let’s talk through what you’d need to do if you want to share your passwords in the event of being incapacitated/passing.

### Test Your Backups

In software, when you have a way to back up a system, the most important thing about a back up is that you test loading from it. Because you don’t want the first test to be when disaster strikes (and when it matters). You want it to be when the stakes are low, and you can sort through the issues.

In this case, the “backup” is the Emergency Access mode. Here’s what you can do for setting this up and testing it out.

1. Set up a family password manager with a trusted person/loved one
2. Allow them to access your account with “Emergency Access”
3. Have them request access to your account via Emergency Access
4. See if they can log into a crucial account (like a bank account, etc.)

A lot of times, these other systems will require access to another factor, e.g. a phone, a key, or code. This can be ANOTHER wrench, and one that’s good to sort through ahead of time. Some options are:

- Adding your partner/trusted friend as someone who can open your phone (share your PIN with them verbally)
- Use a multifactor option that you can use WITHIN the password manager, like [1Password’s TOTP offering](https://support.1password.com/one-time-passwords/) (although this is arguably less secure since it’s not a separate factor, it’s easier to use in the event of an emergency, and as long as you lock the Password manager well, it’s probably OK)
- If you use other forms of multifactor, like a Yubikey, have instructions on how to use the key, and have a backup kept in a safe if you’re worried it’ll be lost

Either way, you’ll want to iron this out and document it in a way that the trusted person knows or can easily find (like a printed document in a safe, or shared as a secure note in the password manager).

### Summary

Password managers give you strong password protection and can be used to access your loved one’s accounts in an emergency or even just via normal sharing. It’s important to be secure but also to be prepared.
