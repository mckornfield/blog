### My Background

I’ve been in the Software + IT industry-10 years, and I’ve probably interviewed 100–1000 people during that time, either via coding screens, panel interviews or simple behavioral interviews.

I’ve seen around that number of resumes (100–1000) as well, and helped some friends edit theirs.

With AI heavily involved in hiring, and entry-level hiring slump, I think a decent resume can only help. Here’s some simple advice.

### 1. Show, don’t tell, with soft skills

I’ve seen a handful of resumes put “great communicator” and list out soft skills in a section. I personally consider that all fluff, because soft skills will show through either:

- By how you conduct yourself in the interview
- By interactions you’ve had with former employees/employers

If you want to show your communication skills or ability to work through difficult problems, describe it with information under your relevant work experience. Show the interviewer *how* you used the skills.

An example, under relevant work experience, might be: “worked with stakeholders through ambiguous requirements to deliver project ahead of schedule.” Two soft skills (navigating ambiguity, managing time) show through without needing their own section.

At least for me, I skip over soft skill sections like that, especially for individual contributor (IC) roles. Keep the soft skills where they belong, off the page and in the interview.

### 2. Understand how resumes are used

I had a coworker that would print out the interviewee’s resume and mark it up, asking questions and clarifications on points made on it. I tried to emulate him by taking notes and asking questions about things that stood out, but I know this isn’t necessarily the norm.

If you’re being interviewed back to back, or by someone who’s very busy, chances are they will only have moments to glance at your resume, they are going to (figuratively) *scan* your resume. In the era of mass submissions, your resume unfortunately serves three purposes:

- Skimmers, who need something that’s easily legible/ laid out
- Reviewers, who will scrutinize each bit of the resume
- Machines, who will scan it and see how it matches with the job

For machines, I’ll talk a bit more in the next section, but for skimmers, you need to have your resume very well laid out. This means clear sections, clear dates, and a decent use of whitespace.

For reviewers, you’re going to want to make sure you have some meat in your resume. Something interesting but also not explicit enough that the interviewer has nothing to ask you.

And for the machines —

### 3. Tailor the resume, but stick to you

This is speculative on my part, but I know *of* the tools that HR uses, though haven’t used them directly. I believe these resume screening tools (also called Applicant Tracking Systems, or ATS) rely on matching your resume content to the job description, using AI or something more rudimentary.

Some people have been using AI to also do the resume matching, leading to all sorts of weirdness/resume spam that has flooded recruiter’s inboxes. While it might help to tailor the resume a bit to the job to get past that initial block, I wouldn’t go through using AI or manually editing your resume for each job.

Instead, I’d tailor the resume to the *type of job* you’re looking for. That means if you’re trying to change jobs, or get something a little outside of your current experience, emphasize work/education experience that’s relevant to the job, and de-emphasize everything else.

If you want to play the game of using an LLM or a tool (or heck, even just your time) to get the same words in a job posting into your resume, be my guest.

But remember that the other sets of people (the recruiters, interviewers and hiring managers) are going to look eventually as well, and they are going to ask you to explain things on your resume, so you *better not lie* (or let an LLM hallucinate)*.*

### 4. Keep it on one page

Listen, some people have been working for a lot longer than me. I’ve only got ~10 years under my belt, but I think getting it all on one page is a great thing to be able to do.

I’ve reviewed the resumes of very senior engineers that have been in the industry for 20 or so years, and they have multi page resumes. But when I flip the page, I go from “Python, ML, Kubernetes” to “PHP, LAMP Stack, XML” and think *do I really care about this project you worked on 15 years ago?*

The part that is stupid about these multi page resumes is something I have seen more than once.

I have asked people about things on the second or third page, and they say “well… that was a while ago.”

*Then don’t 👏 put it 👏 on your resume! 👏*

Here’s what you do: take everything you don’t want to talk about, and put it on your LinkedIn, or put it as a single line on your resume, so we know you were working during that time (because heaven forbid you have a life outside of work). That’ll keep it on one page, even if you’ve worked 20 jobs in 30 years.

Everyone wins when it’s one page:

- It forces you to be concise and focus on what’s relevant to the job
- It lets the scanners actually scan, instead of flipping or scrolling around
- It shows itself as much cleaner than anything that’s 1.5 / 2.5 pages

The resume is a tool, and simple tools are easier to use.

### Summary

1. Show don’t tell us your soft skills
2. Write the resumes for their intended audience (skimmers, reviewers, machines)
3. Tailor the resume to the role, not the job (and don’t lie)
4. Get it to one page, you can do it!
