### Dear Dad, You’re not All Bad

You are not a bad person. I feel I have to get this out first because I tend to focus on the negative, and I don’t want this to be an open hate letter, but one where I try to be fair, in some way.

My dad did good things for me as a kid, and did try. He took me to soccer, despite my inability to play and my crummy attitude, but he really did want me to enjoy something he enjoyed playing.

He took my brother and me to ice skating lessons many times, enough so I learned how to skate and can still make my way across the ice. He also took me skiing for the first time, and took me to see one of the greatest sights I’ve ever seen, the Grand Canyon.

My dad played video games with me, took me to the gym, and in many ways he tried. But this isn’t a story with a happy ending.

Towards the end of my teen years, I was still seeing my dad every other weekend at his my step mom’s place.

It was a pretty awkward time for me personally, but going over there was always peak anxiety for me as a kid. (I once dodged a ski trip I agreed to because I didn’t want to go, and got a nice verbal berating that I won’t forget.)

I never felt comfortable there, and never felt comfortable around his wife or her kids. They were welcoming in their own way, but there were all sorts of things that put me off, like how they treated my severely mentally ill brother, and things like I never had my own space there (I slept in one of my step-sister’s beds, which I always felt weird about as a teenage boy). I also was not very physically active, but that’s basically all they did when I visited (I turned the page on that as an adult, but I was a couch potato of a kid).

All this to say, an awkward relationship made more awkward by my step mom + crew soured towards the end, and I stopped going over to my dad/stepmom’s place.

### A Turning Point

I interacted with my dad minimally through college, and one day after graduating I got mailed a tax bill from him, for selling stock in a custodial account that had my name on it.

Long story-short: my dad stole some money that was “meant for me.” My mom has always done well financially, so this wasn’t a big financial blow, but it made me irrationally angry: we have a crummy relationship, and you go ahead and do this?

This was the second time he’d done *this*; I was just an adult now so my mom got the tax bill the first time. He’d taken my brother’s custodial money as well (he’s a [non-verbal adult](/posts/my-brother-is-a-silent-mirror-of-me/) so he doesn’t need college, but he uses *a lot* of dollars worth of services).

When I talked to my dad on the phone about it the call ended with “what are you going to do, throw your old man in jail?”

I think I should have pushed for *something*.

Misusing a custodial account is a crime, but it was ~$3–5k each time he’d taken it out… it isn’t *nothing*, but easily what it would have been in legal fees.

What I felt from my dad was something really odd. As an adult interacting with another adult, it wasn’t “child abuse” or anything like that. But it was callous and f — d up. It was *entitlement*.

### Three Types of People

The world is three types of people:

- Givers
- Matchers
- Takers

My dad is a taker. One time I saw my step mom hand him an ice pop to hand to someone else, and he just started eating it. I can go on and on but I think that example (plus the above) is pretty cut and dry.

I think part of it is he has a broken brain (the autism/mental illness of my brother and what I have didn’t come from *nowhere*).

But part of it is he is unwilling to make amends or fix things when makes a colossal mistake. I asked him *years* later in a joint virtual therapy session (dystopic I know) if he’d pay me back, and he said he couldn’t, that it was in some fund, or life insurance etc.

The man was also taking nice international trips, so *can’t* is some **bulls — t**.

### Acceptance, with a Catch

The reality is my dad did a bad thing, and won’t take responsibility to fix it, and I can accept that.

I can even find some part of me that forgives him, in that I think he’s mentally not all there, and I understand that broken brains for doing broken things.

**But** no one, in this world, should accept people being careless with them or their feelings. I accept that he did a bad thing (more than once), and that while its financial impact on me is nothing, its emotional impact is pretty tough. I think about it a lot, and it was ten years ago.

To spend any amount of time trying to interact with a person that is willing to do something *against* your interest, and unwilling to make up for it, is to spend time in the clutch of a narcissist.

You are there to benefit *them*.

To this day my dad will send me emails, (which are fine, I read them), but it always makes me feel a little worse at some point, since they talk about me through the lens of *him.*

“Have to also thank you for making me the first grandpa for my class” was in the last email he sent.

You’re welcome?

### An Open Letter to All Dads

To the dads of the world:

Plenty of you do s — y things, and you probably move on with your life. Maybe you get a new family. Maybe you just check out in your own life, hit the bar or the golf course instead of spending time with your kids.

I get it, being a dad is tough: it’s work, and if you do it right, you’ll probably be around for as many good times as bad times.

But I have to ask, as someone who *has* had positive interactions with his dad, to just think about what being a *dad* means. It’s not a sperm donation. It’s not just soccer practice.

It’s providing, helping and supporting your kids. If you’re going to take from your kids, remember that your kids will one day be adults, and those adults won’t want to be near you. I’m sorry you’re lonely, but you reap what you sow.

Every step you take can move you *towards or away* from your kids. If you’re an absentee dad, you’re probably running full steam away from them. If you’re an abusive dad, they’re counting the days until they can escape. And I get you’re broken, and the things you do are [not necessarily intentional](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/48419/this-be-the-verse).

But they do *matter*. Or at least I assume they matter; if anything matters in this world, it’s what you do to others.

You don’t need to have a working brain to know that when someone refuses to interact with you, you’ve done something wrong. You can rationalize it all you want, but remember that being a *dad* means you have children. But once your children make their way into the big wide world, they can make a very important decision:

Are you worth spending time with? Or will you just make me feel s — ty and like I’m hanging out with someone who holds me up like a trophy?

I hope that you’ve made the investment in your kids so that they want to spend time with you, because I know a few dads who haven’t, and I think the dads and their kids are worse for it.

Think before you act. And if you don’t, do your best to make up for it. Otherwise you are leaving this world worse than you found it.

### An Open Letter to Sons

I have little ability to relate to an adult man who has adult children, since I only have a four year old daughter, but I have 32 years of experience as a son, that I think is worth sharing.

The first and most important thing to always say is “[it’s not your fault](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZQht2yOX9Js).” People will treat you s — ty regardless of whether you deserve it.

The second thing is: it doesn’t get better necessarily but it does get more distant. I think about my interactions with my dad a lot, but they don’t weigh on me nearly as much as they used to.

Third: you *are* your father. The more I look in the mirror or think about how I interact with the world, I’m just a bald version of him in many ways (baldness comes from my mother, sadly). I’m sure this varies for people, but I feel I have a pretty strong visual overlap with my dad, as well as a lot of the things we value/ are interested in.

Last point: your decisions are your own. How you treat people is not something you can lay the blame at the feet of any one person in your life, parents included. You are independent, whether you like it or not.

As a son, you must accept what your father did to you (or that he ignored you), that bad things grow distant with time, and that, while you are very much a mirror of the man, you are your own man.
