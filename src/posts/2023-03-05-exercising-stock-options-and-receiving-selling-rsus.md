## Stock Options

### *What are they?*

Options are essentially a fixed price stock option. If you had 100 options to buy Google stock at $10, when you exercised it, you’d only pay $1000 dollars to acquire the stock, even if the stock itself was worth $93.65 as it is today. We’ll get to the importance of that difference later.

Options can be granted before the stock is public (Initial Public Offering, or IPO). If you exercise options pre-IPO, they are kind of in limbo, since you won’t be able to trade them on the open market. Post IPO, exercised options represent stock that can be traded.

### How are they taxed?

Options are kind of strange tax-wise, because you do *pay* for something, so it’s not exactly income. The primary piece that is taxable is when you do eventually sell the stock itself, and depending on the holding period. See the tax section for RSUs below, since it’ll essentially be the same.

What *can* happen when you exercise a share, and the Fair Market Value (FMV) of the option is much higher than the strike/option price, then it might affect the tax you pay by [changing your Alternative Minimum Tax](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc427#:~:text=However%2C%20you%20may%20be%20subject%20to%20alternative%20minimum%20tax%20in%20the%20year%20you%20exercise%20an%20ISO.%20For%20more%20information%2C%20refer%20to%20the%20Instructions%20for%20Form%206251). A lot of websites offer calculators for AMT ([example](https://www.esofund.com/blog/amt-tax)) if you’re worried that exercising your options might affect it.

If you believe that the options will be worth a decent amount in the future, the best time to exercise them is as soon as possible. That will help in the case that you’re looking to sell the stock as capital gains (see below). If you don’t have the money to exercise the shares, then the best time to exercise them is whenever you feel you have the necessary cash.

You can also exercise and sell immediately, if you don’t have the cash on hand (I did this myself), the main drawback though is that everything is taxed as income, which I reiterate below. I’d recommend doing this only if you’re cash strapped and need the cash, or don’t want to worry about owning the stock.

## Restricted Stock Units (RSUs)

### What are they?

RSUs represent stock that a company gives out on some sort of frequency, usually with a vesting schedule that vests some percentage of the total grant over a few years.

Typical schedules look like 25% of the number of granted shares every year for 4 years, or some companies (e.g. [Amazon](https://avieradvisors.com/amazon/amazons-rsu/)) more heavily weigh the latter years, so 5% the first year, and then an increasing number each year.

RSUs are a form of income. They’re worth a certain amount of money when given to you, but you own the stock completely once they vest. If you’re an employee of a company, your main thing to beware are [blackout periods](https://buckheadcapital.com/so-you-have-company-stock-trading-restrictions-blackout-periods-10b5-1-plans/), where selling your stock is illegal.

### How are they taxed?

RSUs are taxed twice. When you receive them, RSUs are essentially income. $5000 worth of stock given to you is like a paycheck for 5000 dollars. Whatever your taxable income is from your employer, just add the amount the RSUs are worth on top to get your taxable income for the year.

The second time RSUs are taxed is in the same way vested options are taxed, which is *a tax on the gain in the asset*. If you received $5000 worth of shares, and in a a year they were worth $10000, you’d be taxed on the capital gain of $5000.

Fortunately, if you hold stock for over a year, that extra $5000 from the share sell is not considered income, but is considered capital gains. Your capital gains tax is generally somewhere between 0–20%.

If you have the need to sell the RSU or exercised options sooner than a one year holding period, you pay tax on the gain as ordinary income, just like when you received the RSU. So the best time to sell them is at a minimum one year later than when you received them. If you want a balanced stock portfolio, it’s probably in your best interest to sell the RSUs after a year and [rebalance](https://www.investopedia.com/investing/rebalance-your-portfolio-stay-on-track/#:~:text=What%20does%20rebalancing%20a%20portfolio,of%20a%20specific%20asset%20class.).
