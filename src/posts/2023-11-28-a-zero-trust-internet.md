The book is intended for any laypeople, but I wrote it because it has everything I’d want to tell my grandpa before he’d gotten hacked.

---

The internet is a pretty amazing place. Founded in the late 60s by research scientists in the southwest, it’s ballooned with the help of the web (the ways that servers are connected) to become one of the most complicated systems humans have ever built. The best visualization I have seen of the internet is in the Disney movie *Ralph Breaks the Internet* (<https://mckornfield.github.io/dont-get-hacked/#ralph> ). The main characters fly around in little pods that bring them to different “sites”, and they run into a set of *mostly* helpful characters, with the exception of a few sinister characters.

In the real world, the movement of these pods can be likened to the data packets that move around the internet. Those “pods” carry your emails, they let you transfer money or pay for goods in a frictionless way. You can have near instant video calls with your loved ones/ friends. You may not see little people hopping in and out of pods, but the result is the same; nearly instantaneous communication and interaction, linking you with the rest of the human world.

One thing that *Ralph Breaks the Internet* gets wrong is the fact that many entities on the internet are *not* your friends. This is caused by a few things:

1. A lot of users (I mean *millions*) on the internet are not real, they are instead bots. Short for “robots”, bots are programs that a malevolent individual controls to serve some malicious purpose, like inflating views, stealing information, or running processes on a machine to steal resources/data
2. Even people on the internet who represent *real* people can either be bots OR people who have compromised an account (e.g. an attacker stole someone’s login information and are now running the account)

I think there have been many terms for this, but the fact is that the internet (and associated media like calls/texting) is an *untrustworthy place.* Don’t let Disney fool you: entities on the internet reaching out to you are, generally speaking, not your friends. Trusting no one is more reliable than trying to weed out who you can and can’t trust.

The power of the internet is near instant information transfer, very well connected systems, and redundancy (i.e. backups).

So if anyone on the internet could be a malicious bot, or a possible anonymous attacker, what should your response be to any attempt to reach out to you?

***Complete Skepticism***.

**Don’t**

**Trust**

**Anyone**

**Online**

Especially not people who are raising some alarm or showing some urgency. Attackers want to scare you, so they will make it sound like “this needs to be done now or there will be consequences!”

Don’t trust someone who *says* they are your grandchild if they send you an email saying they need some gift cards, delivered to some address. **Verify some other way.** Everything on the internet can be compromised, and many things can be faked. Verification from within only the internet can be a difficult task.

But there are some sensible ways to find help and verify.

Reagan said: “Trust but verify,” when dealing with the Soviets. When you get scary things from the internet or your phone, I say “Don’t trust and DEFINITELY verify.” In cyber security, this concept of assuming everyone *could* be an attacker is called *Zero Trust*. It’s a stance I think everyone should take when operating in the internet ecosystem. And verification should come from outside of the attacker’s message (i.e. look up the IRS website, don’t use the links provided in a suspicious email). We’ll cover links in the section on safe web surfing.

And what happens if the government sends you something via an email, phone call, or text, and you don’t reply? Well… it turns out that the government doesn’t operate that way. The FBI has this information (<https://mckornfield.github.io/dont-get-hacked/#fbi>) on their webpage:

> *The FBI will never:*

> *Call or email private citizens to demand payment or threaten arrest. You will also not be asked to wire a “settlement” to avoid arrest.*

> *Ask you to use large sums of your own money to help catch a criminal.*

> *Ask you for wire transfers or gift cards.*

> *Call you about “frozen” Social Security numbers or to coordinate inheritances.*

Or from the IRS’s website (<https://mckornfield.github.io/dont-get-hacked/#irs>):

> *The IRS doesn’t initiate contact with taxpayers by email, text messages or social media channels to request personal or financial information. This includes requests for PIN numbers, passwords or similar access information for credit cards, banks or other financial accounts.*

I’ve received communication from the IRS before about problems with a tax filing, but they contacted me in a way which involved logging into a government system to read the message, not via a cold call, text or email. They also like sending things via regular mail, which hackers tend not to do.

You should have no fear in not responding to these sorts of messages, because the government tends to do things one of three ways:

1. Requiring you to log into a secured system
2. Sending you a piece of legitimate mail
3. Showing up at your door

If you are really concerned that the information you’ve received could be legitimate, feel free to find the non-emergency contact numbers for the FBI/IRS on their website and contact them, they’d prefer that to you getting phished.

Action Items:

- Whenever you see a message from an unknown source, repeat these words in your head: “Don’t Trust Anyone Online.”
- Always verify communications you receive, either by using the company’s/government’s website or help desk phone numbers
