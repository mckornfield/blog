A week in Japan and some history made me think of “Japanese Exceptionalism”

![Japanese perfection, looming in the distance (Himeji Castle, repaired meticulously )](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/0_znOa1PyBuQQS8jaB.jpg)

*Japanese perfection, looming in the distance (Himeji Castle, [repaired meticulously](https://www.kajima.co.jp/english/tech/himeji_castle/index.html))*

I’d read [A Brief History of Japan](https://www.amazon.com/Brief-History-Japan-Samurai-Extraordinary/dp/4805313897) while visiting the island nation, and thought I’d share some of the things I’ve taken away as evidence of what I believe is a “culture of perfectionism.”

All the things I’m going to touch on (trains, cars, toilets, tea, the language, anime/manga) are all products that Japan first *imported* and then *perfected* before exporting or consuming them on their own. Let’s run down the list.

### Trains

![Shinkansen leaving the station 🚅❤️](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/1_jVcOhykWwh5vTBlNdS91Rw.gif.gif)

*Shinkansen leaving the station 🚅❤️*

I’ve ridden a handful of high speed trains: the Acela Express in the Northeastern US, the *AVE* (bird, or spanish high speed) in Spain, and the *Shinkansen* (new main line) in Japan.

What the Spanish and Japanese train lines have in common is they’re both vastly superior to the American train line. And I guess it’s no surprise, since the American train lines were [essentially built by criminals](https://www.amazon.com/Iron-Empires-Robber-Railroads-America/dp/0544770315) (“robber barons”) and have languished as a form of transportation in the face of the car. (Though I’ll get to cars in a second).

But the *Shinkansen* is far and away [the better system](https://www.timeout.com/tokyo/travel/things-you-didnt-know-about-shinkansen-world-famous-japanese-bullet-trains). It’s at most a minute late (the average delay time was 12 seconds?!), there have never been any accidents since its inception in 1957. ([Compare that with Amtrak…](https://nypost.com/2018/02/06/amtrak-train-breaks-apart-at-125-mph/))

Japan’s trains, A+ in my book.

### Cars

![1997 Toyota Prius at the Toyota Museum in Japan](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/0_adoAYOg8dpNCrdvI.jpg)

*1997 Toyota Prius at the Toyota Museum in Japan*

I was talking to my father-in-law about Japanese car standards, and apparently their reliability has a lot to do with requirements on when certain parts of the cars had to be replaced in Japan. Because those standards are more lax in America, these cheaper but more reliable and fuel efficient vehicles ate up the market when they arrived.

Japan’s secret to success is having much [higher fuel efficiency standards](https://www.iea.org/articles/fuel-economy-in-japan) than America (or really any country).

The above 1997 Toyota, almost 30 years old, still has significantly better fuel mileage than most LDV American cars. America’s current average is around 25.4 MPG, this Prius got 46.1 MPG combined (i.e. the average of city and highway).

Inside the Toyota museum, there were plenty of cars, including original Model T’s and other mass produced American cars. America didn’t invent the car either ([it was supposedly Mr. Benz](https://www.loc.gov/everyday-mysteries/motor-vehicles-aeronautics-astronautics/item/who-invented-the-automobile/)), but started mass producing them. Japan took that mass production and created the ideal economy car.

### Toilets

![Behold! Colorful buttons! (Japanese toilet in a hotel room)](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/1_GVvhVpYEciQwoeXtsS1UDA.jpeg.jpg)

*Behold! Colorful buttons! (Japanese toilet in a hotel room)*

[Jim Gaffigan knows it](https://youtu.be/JWV_eAa3sCs?t=65) as well as I do: Japanese toilets are amazing.

You probably know about the bidet features, but did you know they can wash back AND front, the seats themselves are heated, and they can play sounds so people don’t hear your bowel movements?

And these are in public restrooms!

The best bathroom experiences are definitely had in Japan, because they have perfected the toilet. Considering my experience in Chinese bathrooms (holes you squat over), the Japanese toilets appear like space age devices.

### Tea

![Leaf plates!](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/0_P0IXSa6EJ-PTZGot.jpg)

*Leaf plates!*

Matcha is still the preferred way to drink green tea in Japan. It’s this fine powder that you mix into the tea, instead of steeping tea using a tea bag or some tool, the Japanese prefer what Chinese folks would consider a medieval style of tea preparation.

But it tastes pretty good to me. I like the grittiness and it’s pretty low on waste (no tea bags etc. floating around). Instead of innovating on tea preparation, they perfected their 500 year old style.

### Language

![An advertisment, showing off kanji, hiragana and katakana (and English of course)](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/0_qNYwOudzwFK80KA7.jpg)

*An advertisment, showing off *kanji, hiragana and katakana (and English of course)**

Compare the Japanese language with Chinese and you’ll see some similarities (the shared character set is called the *kanji* in Japanese), but they also introduced two phonetic forms over time.

Unlike their Korean counterparts, who now use a purely phonetic alphabet (*hangul*), the Japanese use their *hiragana* and *katakana* alphabets to spell out local and foreign words respectively.

In some sense, you might say “why have three things, instead of just one”. The Chinese just use their character set, and the Koreans use their phonetic one, so why have both?

In some way, I see it as a bit absurd, but in another sense, I see it as the Japanese wanting to preserve their cultural history (*kanji)* while expressing their own individual and easier to learn system (*hiragana/katakana).* To learn pronunciation in Chinese, I’ve only ever seen a western phonetic method (pinyin), whereas you can learn *kanji* pronunciation with a Japanese alphabet. One could say they’re having their cake and eating it too.

### Anime/Manga

![OnePiece Cards and Pokemon Cards, two very successful anime/manga IPs](/img/the-secret-to-japans-success-perfectionism/0_ejAzdbFLJTWVWz3K.jpg)

*OnePiece Cards and Pokemon Cards, two very successful anime/manga IPs*

There’s a reason Anime and Manga I believe are so heavily consumed in America: in many ways they are perfections of storytelling.

While some people might be turned off by the cartoonishness of Anime/Manga, the iconic flashing backgrounds and overly exaggerated emotions help convey character’s feelings and give weight to the story. The Corridor Crew had on an animator to [talk through this idea of “energy”, and reviewed a handful of popular anime](https://youtu.be/BT-nixC0pOA?t=88).

So much of what sticks with you about any media, after years and years, is the *emotions* that the characters have. That stickiness I think is well embodied in Anime.

I remember the explosive anger as well as the blobby, amorphous sadness of characters, from Dragon Ball Z or from Full Metal Alchemist, that I watched as a little kid.

Contrast it with any American kids shows I’ve watched, and they just don’t stick with me the same.

### Summary

Japan has perfected many things, and for that reason they have been super successful! Hopefully you have the opportunity to experience Japan in person as well.

Thanks for reading, hopefully you’re convinced Japan’s perfectionism is the secret to their success.
