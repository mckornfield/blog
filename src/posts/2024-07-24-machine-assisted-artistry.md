### Intellectual Property + Plagiarism

Who owns an idea? Ask Google, [Apple or Samsung](https://www.theverge.com/2018/6/27/17510908/apple-samsung-settle-patent-battle-over-copying-iphone), and they’ll show you the billions of dollars they’ve spent suing each other over idea ownership.

Patents are one form of intellectual property, along with trademarks and copyright, that serve to protect creators (and corporations) from having their creations resold without getting their piece of the pie.

In the ideal, defending intellectual property is one of the greatest protections a service based, “celluloid and silicon” society can do to keep itself on top. The United States efficiently grows lots of food, but its post industrial society is one where intellectual property remains one of the few advantages it maintains over the rest of the world.

And what better form of intellectual property than the bleeding edge, GPU and TPU based Machine Learning models, capable of generating images, songs and reams of “written” text.

Well, maybe this is where we’ve created our own demons.

### Machine Learning vs. Intellectual Property

A Machine Learning model can be based off more than one algorithm, though the deep learning/ large language model variety are responsible for the recent takeoff in AI applications. These models, no matter the outputs they produce, rely on one mechanism.

Lots and lots of curated, labeled, and *quality* data.

“Labeled” in this sense means data that has a clear identifier. If you want to look at one of the earliest successes of the most recent wave of deep learning models, you might think it was some amazing advancement in the field of computing or hardware. While the hardware has gotten better ([and Nvidia is all the richer for it](https://companiesmarketcap.com/nvidia/marketcap/)), and the software supporting ML applications has improved, the success comes from creating these high quality training data sets, e.g. [ImageNet](https://www.image-net.org/), 14+ *million* hand labeled images.

But quality data and data labelling aren’t free. ImageNet and other data sets like it are curated by humans. Silicon Valley’s giants are riding the AI wave, but they’re doing so on the backs of data created and curated by other sources (like Reddit or StackOverflow).

The fuel for ChatGPT, DALL-E and all of the other tools of this ML revolution are these data. And that data can very quickly step into murky IP territory.

If you ask ChatGPT to write you a paragraph in the style of Steven King, or DALL-E to draw you as Captain America, these tools will use the insights they’ve gained to commit *Intellectual Property Theft,* relying on insights they took from source material.

If you published content online on a site that was hoover-ed up by the training sets for these models, you’ve contributed to a corpus of knowledge that the model can draw from. But… how is this different than how a person would learn?

### You Don’t Need ML to Plagiarize

One of the first known trademarks in the West (i.e. you marked the item of your trade) was stamping bread in [1266](http://www.tmprotect.idknet.com/eng/history.html). This was a time where people mostly walked or rode horses, and where illiteracy was rampant.

If you had a state-of-the-art data center and ML model back then, you wouldn’t be able to find much data to train it on, besides texts preserved by the various empires of the time, and none of it in a digital format… so the intellectual property laws of the time were about the things that like different bakeries.

The key point of this trademark was: if you produced bread and tried to pass it off as the bread of another baker, you could be brought into court.

As corporations have evolved from ways to [insure people against merchant ships sinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporation#:~:text=Dutch%20and%20English%20chartered%20companies%2C%20such%20as%20the%20Dutch%20East%20India%20Company%20%28VOC%29%20and%20the%20Hudson%27s%20Bay%20Company%2C%20were%20created%20to%20lead%20the%20colonial%20ventures%20of%20European%20nations%20in%20the%2017th%20century) to legally backed, cash rich entities, the trademark and other forms of intellectual property protections have grown to become fearsome forces in the legal world.

All of this happened before there were any forms of artificial intelligence. As long as there have been concepts you can profit from stealing, there have been people there to steal them.

The lines can be blurry, just ask —

### Blurred Lines and the Problem of Music IP

One of the silliest examples of IP theft is the [lawsuit surrounding the Marvin Gaye estate and Robin Thicke’s song “Blurred Lines”](https://ethicsunwrapped.utexas.edu/case-study/blurred-lines-copyright). If you want a better example of the murkiness surrounding this issue, the lawsuit was won because:

> In March 2015, the jury ruled in favor of the Gaye estate, stating that while Williams and Thicke did not directly copy “Got to Give It Up,” there was enough of a similar “feel” to warrant copyright infringement.

I’m not a musician, but, isn’t all music inspired by something before it? Novelty in itself is not very interesting.

I can randomly hit notes on a guitar, or drag my hand across a keyboard, but I’m not going to start raking in royalties.

Music is a very constrained field. You have to obey rhythms, scales, and harmonies, and so you end up with people [reusing progressions that sound good](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JdxkVQy7QLM).

I know for a fact [many artists have deliberately reused or integrated others’ music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Led_Zeppelin_songs_written_or_inspired_by_others) into their own. The constraints are not just technically what is possible in a musical sense, but what is someone *willing to listen to*.

### Why is ML Knowledge Any Different?

You could say the same about borrowing/inspiration for any form of intellectual property

- software (so much of production software relies on open source, let alone people pasting code from the web)
- visual content (art styles mean you are copying *how* someone draws something)
- written content (if I use iambic pentameter and reference “fair Verona,” I’m probably copying Shakespeare)

Part of what makes knowledge *useful* is that it’s based on the success of those that have deployed it before. Sure, you could come up with new ways to construct intellectual property from the ground up, but you’ll be way behind those who try to emulate the best and create their own content.

Turning back to these ML models, what makes their knowledge so radically different from that of accomplished artists and other *intellectual property generators*? That it’s etched onto silicon instead of neuronal mass?

I think there’s really only one difference that matters —

### This is a Battle Between Corporations/Ultra Rich Individuals

Ideally these intellectual property violations would be similar to those that happen in the normal human world. If you produce content that violates IP laws, you get sued, regardless of whether you used an ML model or created the content yourself.

The difference is that ML models aren’t just being used by individuals, they’re being deployed by the largest market cap companies in the world. As Google, Apple and Samsung have shown by suing each other to oblivion, Comcast and AT&T can get a piece of the pie by rolling out armies of lawyers and taking aim at those IP stealing ML companies.

### Who’s Not Protected

The reality is, no individual is protected from this data gobbling onslaught. Corporations might laud the fact that they’ll protect your data or keep you safe, but it’s the way they’ve commodified your existence, selling you ads and making addictive goods and services commonplace.

Companies might decry AI’s blatant IP theft, but only so they can load up their coffers. It has nothing to do with protecting the individual.

What can we do to protect the individual? Maybe we need more —

### Democratic AI, Democratic Content

What we need isn’t more policy or laws to get rid of all the thieves, but to embrace the fact that this theft is part and parcel of human existence. We need to build higher walled gardens, sure, but what we really need is a democratization of this force, so that the haters AND the lovers can make better art.

Instead of banning AI and concretizing the (supposed) holiness of AI-**un**assisted human artistry, we should instead empower ourselves to be better artists by using AI when it helps us.

What we need for democratic AI is what we probably need across the board: the dismantling of ultra powerful corporations who squish creators under their boots. This means breaking up big tech (the usual suspects) AND big music (i.e. Sony, Universal and Warner).

Instead of corporations using our data as their right, and using our work as their privilege, we should take both back and make them sign the checks.

Platforms like Medium, or self publishing/blogging, can help combat this. Owning our own destinies and making people pay when they use our data or our art (generated WITH or WITHOUT AI) will be the way out of this IP theft quagmire.

I think the best analogy to leave with is:

When [chess robots beat humans](https://www.ibm.com/history/deep-blue#:~:text=Deep%20Blue%20%7C%20IBM&text=In%201997%2C%20IBM%27s%20Deep%20Blue,match%20under%20standard%20tournament%20controls.), [humans use robots to beat *those* robots](https://new.uschess.org/edwards-32nd-ICCF-ch#:~:text=But%20I%20would%20argue%20that%20it%E2%80%99s%20not%20just%20about%20the%20hardware%2C%20but%20also%20about%20one%E2%80%99s%20ability%20to%20make%20the%20most%20of%20the%20hardware).

We don’t need more restrictive laws, or more greedy companies; those will just serve higher level interests.

We need more empowered **creators**, leading to a virtuous cycle of creativity, innovation and artistry.

Empowered creators who create art using the work of the greats to stand on, the best technologies of the time to assist them, and their own minds to call forth something *novel* and *compelling* will be the creators who beat the unassisted creators and the ML models.

And hopefully, they’ll beat the corporate vultures too.
