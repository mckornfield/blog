Why going around can beat going up

Each of my moves sideways taught me valuable things that simply staying in the same role doesn’t.

### **Get Paid to Learn**

Moving jobs or roles is about enriching yourself. In my experience, most job roles go like so:

- 0–3 months — Learn a lot, do relatively little
- 3–6 months — Learn some, do more
- 6–9 months — Learn little, do a lot
- 9–12 months — Learn almost nothing, do do do

I have found that within a year of the same job, with the same roles and responsibilities, especially on the same work area, if your head isn’t up your a$$, you will probably stop learning.

Moving up the jungle gym means you stop learning, even if you keep learning. Moving over means you get to learn, and ultimately, you get *paid* to learn, which is truly win-win. If you can move over within a company, you can also keep your options/RSUs, whereas you’ll lose them if you move companies.

Speaking of earning, one thing I found was —

### **Moving Over Can Mean More $$$**

Companies are stupid this way. If you stay in your role, you’ll find that folks get hired in and are making the same as you or more. This is frustrating for two reasons:

1. Based on my rule of 0–12 months, the new hire will spend 3–6 months learning instead of producing
2. If you have to train them, the new person is a tax on *your* time

I have limited experience, granted, but I’ve found that businesses *overvalue* tenure elsewhere and *undervalue* tenure internally. They do this by giving lame raises and boring work to their current employees (forcing them to go elsewhere)

### **Domain Specific Knowledge is Great for a Company, but Terrible for an Employee**

Businesses live and die by their domain specific knowledge; i.e., how does this specific software subsystem work, what needs do our customers have, etc.

This is unfortunately all knowledge that has to be largely abstracted to appear on a resume, and in an interview/hiring setting, you don’t get points for knowing the ins and outs of a custom built system. Companies want to hire employees who know fancy frameworks and toolsets, not who have a bunch of domain specific knowledge.

Remember my 0–12 month range? I think that as an addendum to this, around the 6–12 month range, you start to ingest a lot more domain specific knowledge. This is knowledge that helps you do your job better, but is ultimately not transferrable.

***A running analogy***

To give an analogy (and a bit of an aside, so bear with me), imagine you run the same 3 miles every day.

You will teach yourself the mechanics of running and also build up the endurance to run the distance. You might even get faster, shortening the time it takes you to cover the distance.

But after a while, your body will get used to the same stimulus. You’ll even get used to things like the pedestrian/traffic flow, or knowing which cracks and crevices to avoid tripping over. You’ll become *very* good at running that specific route.

If you do this same thing for years and years, you’ll become so good at running that one route that it’ll be a breeze.

But if I drop you at the beginning of another route, or ask you to run FARTHER, you might take a while to become comfortable, tripping and stumbling over unfamiliar terrain.

On the other hand, you’ll have the endurance to pound out those miles. That’s because the knowledge you have about that specific route is *not* transferrable, but the skill you build up to run it is*.*

Domain specific knowledge is knowing the exact contours of that route (i.e. your company’s specific implementation of data services, it specific service endpoints), while transferrable skillsets (like working with different data technologies, or running RESTful or gRPC services) are the endurance and mechanics you’ve cultivated.

Both are necessary to do a job well, but, to go back to the analogy, good runners can run anywhere, meaning they have enough of a good running skillset to trump their lack of intimate knowledge of a route. As can good employees can thrive anywhere.

### Companies Aren’t Loyal, Why Should You Be?

In my other article I mentioned that some people view changing jobs/ positions frequently as “job hopping.” I think that can be a negative if what people are doing is building something terrible, trying to sweep it under the rug, and move away.

But I think moving jobs shouldn’t be about leaving behind garbage. Ultimately I believe in follow through and leaving things in a state at least similar to what you found them in, hopefully a bit improved.

The real truth here is that, no company gives a damn about you. Unless your name is on the business, you’re not part of a family. Every contract I’ve signed to work for a business has legalese to the tune of “you can quit whenever you want, and we can fire you whenever we want.”

If a company can find a better worker to do your job, they’ll fire you.

If you work for a part of the company they consider a cost all of a sudden, they’ll lay you off.

Maybe companies valued workers at one point, but I think every person should view *themselves* as a business (i.e., the “solopreneur”). Being a solopreneur is usually about being a one person business, but I think it means a handful of things:

1. Learning is how you enable yourself to earn more
2. Do what you can to increase your pay
3. — While decreasing the work you hate
4. Business is business, not family
5. Find ways to earn outside of your 9–5

Unless your family name is on the business, you have no “business” being loyal.

I’m not saying “be lazy and scam money out of your employer because f — the system.”

I’m saying “work hard, *and* keep your head up for a better opportunity.”

### Summary

1. Getting paid to learn is great. Moving around the jungle gym means you get to learn more
2. Sometimes a move over is both a move over AND up (in terms of total comp)
3. Domain specific knowledge isn’t the best for your career
4. Companies don’t deserve your loyalty
