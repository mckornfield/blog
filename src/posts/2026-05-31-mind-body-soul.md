### Core Wounds

For me growing up, there were two obvious wounds in my life

1. An early divorce (don’t remember parents being together)
2. My brother being severely mentally ill

I’ve written about the negatives plenty of times, but I want to turn to a more positive view of myself, through the lens of my parents, in hopes that you too might see yourself as the combination of the mind, body and soul of your parents.

### Mind

My dad is a sharp guy. One of the ways that he demonstrates his sharpness is humor (past the point of social acceptability, sure, but that’s sort of the point of humor). I’ve always thought he was funny, and I always try to be humorous (though as previous stated, past the point of acceptability).

When I met my wife I told her my favorite dumb bar joke (which… I learned playing *World of Warcraft*):

> A pirate walks into a bar with a steering wheel lodged into his pants

> The bartender says “What is that steering wheel doing in there?”

> The pirate responds: “Arrrrrrrrrr, it be drivin’ me nuts!”

*&lt;Laughter Sign>*

Anyway… she thought it was funny.

There is much more to a mind than humor, but I’m the beneficiary of a mind that, unlike my brother’s, allows me to make a decent living, make silly jokes, and have interesting conversations.

This mind I owe for sure to my dad, and as well to my mom, who is herself a voracious reader and operates with a level of executive function I have not seen matched in the world.

Minds are fickle things. And while many of the things we learn are skills, our capacities and interests most certainly come from our parents, both biologically and behaviorally, since they are the ones who raise us.

### Body

I am in much better shape now than when I was younger. One of the tougher parts of the divorce is that my physically active dad separated from my not so physically active mom, so the part of me that yearned to be physically active languished.

The body/mind problem is one that’s been argued about over the centuries, but my personal experience is that we ignore either *at great peril.* I’ve got into running long distances, which is good because it’s simple (put on the shoes and GTFO) and tough (I still am out of breath running up a big old hill). But most importantly, it gives me a time to think away from the quotidian, screen based environment I find myself in.

My dad in his 50s is still playing hockey, despite being told numerous times to stop because of injuries etc.

But f- the doctors, keep moving until (or?) you die.

My mom recently has been using GLP-1s and feels great because of it, and looks great. Having had 4 kids, her body has been through the ringer. She’s been working on her strength ability at the gym and really slimmed down. I was an off and on chubby kid through elementary and middle school, and I f — ing hated myself just because I was a gross chubby kid.

I can’t change the person I was, but I can accept that he didn’t have the capacity to be any different than he was: a kid chronically online who did little in the physical world. I’m thankful that I can channel the active part of me now as an adult; it’s made me a lot more sane. And I know I have my dad’s genes to thank for it.

### Soul

Do I even have a soul?

For most of my youth and adulthood, I would have considered myself “no faith” or atheist. Even now as I write, I’m still pretty unsure, but I like the idea I’ve heard from various speakers, which is: it’s good to act like you and others *do* have souls.

Does God exist?

I also don’t think so. But I think it’s good to live as though you think he *does* exist.

Neither of my parents have struck me as very religious, but both lean pretty liberal and are well educated, so they check the most obvious non-religious boxes. My mom has always been spiritual, and I’m pretty sure my dad venerates the New York Giants, but I don’t think either of those things are what I’m looking for.

My mom though I do think has a good soul. She has always been generous with her time and finances, giving to families and charities well beyond what is required of her. I hope to be able to mirror her kindness in some way, even if I’m financially not at her level.

Most importantly, I have issues with my dad that are still pretty unresolved. But I’m hoping a turn to faith might help me deal with those issues, turning what has been a difficult relationship into something I have the grace to process.

I’ve read recently the best revenge is not being the person who has wronged you. I’m doing my best to be a good father to my own kid, and to try and help other families or kids the little bit that I can, to try and right the core wounds I have and carry with me.

Thanks for reading and I hope you can appreciate the mind, body and soul your parents may or may not have given you.
