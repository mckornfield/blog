And who it’s actually for

![Photo by Kelly Sikkema on Unsplash](/img/why-agile-persists/0_F-98-BaYOdpnHVtd.jpg)

*Photo by [Kelly Sikkema](https://unsplash.com/@kellysikkema?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)*

If you’ve never heard of agile, it’s a software development principle that’s been [declared dead](https://medium.com/developer-rants/agile-has-failed-officially-8136b0522c49) many times and is lampooned by many developers in the industry as being a waste of time. It has a [set of principles](https://medium.com/informationartist/agile-manifesto-explained-visually-d2fb07211373) that exist to distance it from Waterfall (the traditional project management technique still common in manufacturing), but I won’t repeat them here.

Just imagine meetings every week or two to discuss short term increments (Sprints), planning meetings on a similar cadence, stand up meetings every day to talk over tickets on a board, and you have most of the broad brushstrokes of Agile.

While developers might dislike it, here’s why it will continue to exist.

### It’s Not for Developers… It’s for Everyone Else

Developers primarily interact with a system by using coding tools (GitHub, IDEs) and by interacting with cloud deployment tools (Terraform, cloud consoles, CI/CD pipelines). All of this information is generally more useful than the analogues Agile operates on, Stories (small increments) and Epics (big increments).

But people that aren’t developers (managers, product, quality) don’t use those coding tools. They don’t have boots on the ground, either because they don’t have the technical expertise or the time.

So having *something* that the non-developers can use to communicate a shared understanding with the developers becomes more the purpose of Agile than anything around developer productivity.

And for developers, this means in addition to the places they actually do their work, they also become ticket jockeys, writing up stories and pushing them around a virtual board. So productive!

### It Really Helps Managers

Software Development is a fuzzy discipline. It can be really impossible to tell what work people are doing as so much of the work is a combination of design and implementation. In manufacturing, these roles tend to be divided (design engineers vs. process technicians), but in software, one person will design AND implement.

This makes it difficult for managers to tell what the heck developers are doing. Someone might throw the word “refactoring” around, or talk about “getting tests to pass,” but this has no bearing on what a manager would care about (namely, is this person just sitting on their hands?)

But having cards that sit on a board, and cards that do (or don’t) move around, is a great (if sometimes false) analogue for progress. The number of closed tickets, or story points, or whatever other metric used to measure velocity, gives managers the (probably false) confidence that things are getting done! I remember very well that I felt our Agile Coach (the person who facilitates all of the Agile nonsense) was simply a way for our boss to keep his finger on the pulse.

### And… Developers can Abuse it too

I said the word *false* in parenthesis a couple times in the above paragraph, and I think it applies if developers are abusing agile. If you’re a smart (or evil, or lazy) developer, you can definitely game the system by making lots of small tickets that move through quite quickly (this is, in fact, what Agile wants you to do). But they could essentially be busy work, or not actually map to real value for a user of the software.

Developers can hide behind tickets and points as cover for not doing anything meaningful. All systems can be abused, and Agile is no different.

### Working Software is not that Iterative

Software that works is usually a painful back and forth process, but not an iterative one. What I mean by *iterative* is that you can release a version, and then release an improvement to that version (this is the main promise of software over things like firmware/hardware).

But the first version of something cannot be iterative. It has to work, well enough that a customer will want to use it, and not fall apart on some amount of data or processing.

This means that instead of an Agile approach, initial versions of software tend to be more Waterfall (or [Water-Scrum-Fall](https://cloudcoach.com/blog/a-guide-to-water-scrum-fall-project-management/)), and take much longer cycles to produce.

I don’t think this is a bad thing either, this is just reality.

### To Sum It Up

Agile serves non-developers (especially managers) much more than developers, unless those developers seek to abuse it as something to hide behind. And since the first version of a piece of working software tends not to be that iterative, the Sprint (or Kanban) based nature of Agile becomes a rhythm, that ultimately feeds a Waterfall like process.

The only people who really feel the squeeze are frustrated developers who feel the process doesn’t add much, or frustrated product managers who aren’t getting anything out of developers who abuse the system. The only way to get by is to [continue making fun of it](https://javascript.plainenglish.io/20-hilarious-memes-for-those-who-have-worked-in-the-agile-framework-e6c17b4d8260).
