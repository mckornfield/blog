### Thought Work over Physical Work

I listened to Trevor Noah’s [recent podcast with Sam Altman](https://open.spotify.com/episode/2WSPcEbWAj5XvAlXd0Cyxa), the CEO of OpenAI. If you don’t know who these people are, it’s one of South Africa’s funniest (and probably smartest, nuts to Elon) people talking with the face of the AI revolution, the man who runs the company that makes ChatGPT.

They talked at multiple levels about AI (technology, society, capitalism), but what stuck out to me is the decision Sam made early on in the company.

OpenAI early on had a robotics division, but Altman realized that there were limitations to robotics AI, namely the speed they could improve at. His reasoning was *if we build an AI that can be as good as/better than us at thought tasks, it could help us build better robots*. It is also a lot faster to iterate (i.e. improve) with software than with hardware/firmware changes.

Altman believes that ChatGPT will only get better and better, as well as specialized large language models (LLMs) that other companies are building. But these are simply ways to replace thought workers, so what about physical work?

### The Blue Collar Boom

The wage gap is shrinking between [college educated and high school graduates](https://www.economist.com/leaders/2023/11/30/a-new-age-of-the-worker-will-overturn-conventional-thinking), thanks in part to an AI revolution that seeks to replace college education with prompts any literate person can ask. You can teach an LLM many things, but not effectively how to turn a wrench, at least not yet.

No one is calling in the robot welders or oil rig workers, though they are seriously considering AI for all sorts of thought work, like dealing with [40%+ of legal work](https://www.economist.com/business/2023/06/06/generative-ai-could-radically-alter-the-practice-of-law). Self driving cars/trucks were all the rage a bit ago, but the reality is, hardware/firmware systems are messy, and people getting killed ([even if someone is in the car](https://www.theverge.com/2023/7/31/23814474/uber-self-driving-fatal-crash-safety-driver-plead-guilty)) is terrible PR.

If a truck driver kills someone, they’re at fault, but if a self driving car does with no backup driver, it’s technology/engineers/a company’s fault.

A self driving car, or a [robot construction worker](https://bostondynamics.com/), has to navigate a complex 4D world, one that humans struggle to handle too. It will be a tall order to engineer one of these machines well enough to blanket replace all (or *any)* blue collar workers.

If we want to build all of our green energy revolution and chips in the US, we’re going to need [a lot of humans turning screws](https://www.bluegreenalliance.org/site/9-million-good-jobs-from-climate-action-the-inflation-reduction-act/), not typing. Globalization might have screwed a lot of American blue collar workers in the recent past, but populism and climate activism is going to put them back in the (literal and figurative) driver’s seat.

The jobs that are going to go away, it turns out, are the ones that involve lots of typing.

### The Thought Worker Bust

2022–2023 is the year of the Thought Worker bust. Startups and big tech all looked at their budgets, and decided 100s of thousands of employees they paid to stare at screens and enter text into those screens weren’t necessary.

In many ways, they’re totally right. So much of the thought work that’s performed in the world is ancillary. We the thought workers might rant and rave about how we’re “changing the world” and “making impact.”

But a lot of times we’re discussing how to build a better system (that no one is going to use anyway) or how to better organize code that was never necessary in the first place.

But how big do you think OpenAI is, a company that is at the center of this revolution?

~800 people

So you can build one of the greatest tech innovations with a small American town (and granted I’m sure they used cloud services, open source, etc. but at this point those are practically utilities).

Fewer than 800 people built something that 100s of millions of people use every day.

Compare that with giants like Alphabet (Google) or Microsoft, whose employee counts sit in the ~200,000 range, but who I think most people would consider more like utility companies than innovative tech firms. And, based on the [recent tech layoffs](https://news.crunchbase.com/startups/tech-layoffs/#:~:text=How%20many%20recent%20tech%20layoffs,were%20laid%20off%20in%202022.), these utility companies would consider themselves bloated.

Between OpenAI and the MAAMAs there are plenty of mid sized or *over*sized companies technology companies, with lots of thought leaders and many of them are not profitable, hence the bust.

You don’t really need that many people to build something great, you just need smart, dedicated people and a good mission. And as the tooling and scalability improves, the number of thought workers we’ll need in tech and beyond will most likely diminish.

### Great Tools for Everyone

In the podcast, Altman made a point that his company (and others like it) are trying to give humans great tools to build the world of tomorrow.

It’s an amazing prospect, to think that people that don’t have millions of dollars worth of servers at their disposal can ask a hyperintelligent AI to help them solve a problem, or build a company. I’ve used Github Copilot recently and it is [scary good at suggesting blocks of code](https://github.blog/2023-05-17-inside-github-working-with-the-llms-behind-github-copilot/) (with some caveats of course).

I can easily imagine a few years down the road where most software engineers are simply asking machines to generate code that *does a thing*, and then making sure it fits nicely into a code generated ecosystem. More like software managers/architects than software engineers.

I’ll always remember my old boss ranting about how he was looking forward to the day where he could replace us all with a bot to write the code for him. In this “great tools for everyone” world, my old boss is a one person organization that employs 1000s of AI coders to drum up the software he dreams about.

### The Human Body Revolution

If we fast forward to a time where robotics are good enough to step in for our blue collar workers, we’ll still need people who know how to turn the wrench properly, to make sure our AI construction robots aren’t making a mess out of things. But once again, we’ll have construction managers overseeing a team of robots. And in the team up until then, the might of the physical worker will only grow as our machines become more complicated and the infrastructure supporting them becomes more widespread.

What used to be 100s of humans employed in standing up a skyscraper could be a handful of onsite humans directing a band of robots.

And what will the rest of our blue collar workers be doing? They’ll be building structures on the Moon and on Mars with their robot assistants.

And the thought workers?

I guess we’ll see... Maybe we’ll have to trade our white collars for blue ones.
