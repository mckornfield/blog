### The First Mechanized “Computers”

A history lesson on computers would be remiss without mentioning who the first *computers* were.

Women.

Women who were very good at doing complicated math by hand. These women would compute things like the trajectories of spacecraft or ordinance fired by military craft. If you haven’t seen *Hidden Figures*, it’s worth it because it gets at the divide between the “scientists” (men) and the “computers” (women, and black women in this specific instance).

While these women were effective, there was always the desire to go *faster*, and advances in machines resulted in the first mechanized computers.

Warehouse sized computers, like ENIAC and the Mark II. Their logic units were these big boxy elements called vacuum tubes, a pre-silicon transistor that allowed these computers to simply do what the women computers before them had done, albeit a bit faster.

They worked with an existing input/output system, the punch card. This hard to use piece of technology is why standard terminal sizes are only 80 characters, as this was the [number of columns a punch card had](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card).

Women were still heavily involved in the computing, since they operated all of these mechanized computers. Grace Hopper is famous for creating what would become the COBOL language, but also for operating one of the first computers, the UNIVAC I.

### From Mainframes to PCs and Smartphones

The 70s and 80s brought the transition of computers as large machines filling a warehouse to block but much more manageable machines for personal use. People had Apple II’s, and Microsoft DOS, or the Commodore 64 as options all of a sudden, instead of needing access to a mainframe to get involved in the world of computers.

In War Games, Matthew Broderick uses an [IMSAI 8080](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMSAI_8080) to kick off the “war games” with the WOPR computer, using a personal computer to initiate a near catastrophic series of events with a mainframe computer.

The power of personal computers cannot be overstated; they have dramatically changed how people work and interact throughout the world. So much of business is now conducted on these machines (especially in the form of tablets/ smartphones), that it’s hard to remember that a few generations ago, paper and ink were the tried and true media for conducting business.

Smartphones represented a step farther (or smaller), where computers could now be carried on our person, buzzing and nagging us.

This period of time also saw a major misfortune, as [women were pushed out of the field](https://www.theguardian.com/careers/2017/aug/10/how-the-tech-industry-wrote-women-out-of-history) and replaced by men, as software related labor was seen as less fitting for a woman. You mean the work they had been doing all along? OK.

### Back to Mainframes Again

The rise of the web in the 90s empowered servers once again. They were much smaller this time, but the demand for a large volume of them to run all sorts of different workloads surged as eCommerce and online entertainment rose to eclipse traditional retail and cable TV.

A book selling company, Amazon, grew to be one of the largest server farm maintainers (AKA cloud providers) on the planet, running around 30% of the internet today.

The difference between a “cloud” and a mainframe I think is simply a manner of degree. The mainframe was one giant machine that you could get some time on, where the cloud is a highly complex network of lots of smaller but much more powerful computers. The processing power of one of the smallest cloud VMs is orders of magnitude more than any of the mainframes from the 50s or 60s.

But at the same time, so much of how computers used to work comes into play in the cloud world of today. Systems are not just decentralized, sure, but they are not *local*. Things are happening on some machines located in a large facility, one that exists to maintain a large, multi-purpose technical system.

While the complexity and scale have increased, the core features of how we interact with the system remain largely the same. Developers interact with cloud systems using terminals that can be bigger than 80 characters, but look so similar, it doesn’t matter. Virtualization, containers and other abstractions have made dealing with these computing systems more resilient to failures, but also made them even more complicated.

We’ve replaced the complexity of operating a punch-card setup and a large vacuum tube machine with cloud management software, serverless technology and Kubernetes, and probably most complex of all, multi-zonal and multi-regional support. The mainframes of our past were maybe harder to operate, but had much simpler footprints and usecases they supported.

While things have changed, in many ways, things still stay the same. And funnily enough, there are plenty of mainframes in use, including ones that are [still being made today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Z#IBM_z14).

The main disparity is not in how the systems are set up, but instead, on the developers, who as I mentioned are now mostly men. We can only hope that a more inclusive workforce and education system brings more women computer science and tech professionals back to the field they helped to pioneer.
