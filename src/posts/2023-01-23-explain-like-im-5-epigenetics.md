### A tale of two biologists

Imagine you’re a biologist in the year 1700. You see lots of animals in the world, many of them very different than other animals. There’s especially a good amount of variety among the animals in the human world, like domesticated dogs species and all their breeds.

You’re a biologist so you want to try and understand WHY they’re different, you study a bunch of animals (lets say dogs) and you come to some conclusions:

- Animals that exhibit certain behaviors/traits have offspring that also have similar behaviors/traits
- An animals skill and ability honed over the course of its life influences its offspring

Now imagine I take you, the year-1700 biologist, and ask you “why do giraffes have long necks?”

Your answer can basically come down to

- Long necked giraffes have long necked kids, if it’s something beneficial those kids will do better

OR

- Early giraffes who stretched their necks more had children with longer necks

These are basically the competing theories of evolution that monsieurs Darwin and Lamarck respectively came up with.

Lamarck: Giraffes have long necks because their ancestors trained their necks to be longer and passed it on

Darwin: Giraffes were selected by environmental pressures (taller trees, enhanced survival with necks as a weapon?) to have long necks, so certain giraffe ancestors died out and others lived and had children

The result of the 19th and most of the 20th century was a set of victories for Darwin. Darwinian evolution, the theory of natural selection, became a cornerstone of modern biology. Along with…

### The Genome

Darwin really had no clue what exactly passed information from parent to offspring, but he was followed up by a line of scientists that did a lot of chemistry to narrow down the exact mechanism.

Most people have heard of DNA, or “the genome,” but it’s the set of information that carries those traits from parent to offspring. Once it was discovered, it was considered a major breakthrough in showing the mechanism of Darwinian evolution. Something along the lines of:

- Genes mix within organisms that exchange genes (i.e. ones that sexually reproduce)
- Those mix of genes provide differences (variation)
- Those variations are SIMILAR to the parents, but DIFFERENT enough to, over time, produce *significant* variations
- Any significant enough variations that are good for the organism mean those genes continue, while other types of variations are either ignored or go extinct

The example of Genghis Khan spreading himself and his progeny throughout Asia is a [good example](https://www.iflscience.com/fact-check-are-one-in-200-people-descended-from-genghis-khan-65357#:~:text=How%20many%20children%20did%20Genghis,an%20estimated%2016%20million%20descendants.). He was good at killing, procreating, and not being killed, very important traits in male genes.

But having the exact chemistry figured out is a pretty convincing point for Darwin… So Lamarck can pack up right? We got DNA, we have the understanding of why certain genes outperform others.

Neck stretching: 0, Longer necked variation: 1000?

Well…

### The Epi-genome

The sad but beautiful part about science is that it is constantly invalidating its own findings. If there are data that don’t support your theories, then you either have bad data, or bad theories. And over time, the more data you have, the more likely you are to know if your data is bad.

The genome is information on how to build certain things, like “make a long neck”, or “make a fuzzy tail.” But it’s very inert, it doesn’t exactly express HOW those things work. And many things can influence how those blueprints work.

Example: Boys and girls have the same chromosomes except for one Y chromosome in boys, vs. two in girls. Why are boys so visibly different from that one chromosome, if they have the other?

The thing about genes is that their *expression* is very different depending on the other factors. We can think of these other factors like the construction crew that works in your body. They spend their time deciding which blueprints to read, and which ones to ignore.

They also do things like spill coffee on the blueprints (methyl groups) making them harder for other members of the construction crew to use. These methyl groups work simply by sticking to the DNA in different spots, either making it easier or harder to read certain segments. Though there is a lot to the epigenome, these methyl groups are one of the main things you can pass on to offspring.

### How you can change the epigenome

Here are some interesting examples of epigenetics at work:

*Mice licking their babies ->* Mice mothers who [lick their babies](https://radiolab.org/episodes/261176-the-great-mother-switcheroo) produce epigenetic changes in their offspring that encourage those babies to lick their children. But they also grow up to be a lot less stressed out, so it’s not just handing down a set of licks.

*Smokers ->* The children of smokers (either their mom while in the womb or mom and dad before conception) have a [higher increased risk of addiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigenetic_effects_of_smoking#:~:text=Cigarette%20smoking%20has%20been%20found,between%20smokers%20and%20non%2Dsmokers.) and negative health outcomes as infants.

*Starving during puberty ->* Remarkably, fathers who [were in starvation conditions around the time of puberty](https://content.time.com/time/subscriber/article/0,33009,1952313-1,00.html) give birth to descendants who have longer lifespans. This kind of leads into my favorite…

*The Lifespan Book ->* David Sinclair lays out a ton of ways you can affect your genome, from heat, cold, stress and (intermittent) starving, to advanced chemistry, this book is one of the best studies on how to increase your lifespan by changing your epigenome. It focuses on “the survival circuit,” something a bit more advanced than our friends the methyl groups.

### Summary

- Darwin and Lamarck turned out to be both right, but they never understood the exact mechanism ([I don’t think Lamarck was right about giraffes though…](https://www.quantamagazine.org/can-darwinian-evolution-explain-lamarckism-20170511/))
- DNA is the blueprint (genome), the epigenome is the way the blueprint gets used
- You can pass on advantages (and disadvantages) within a generation, beyond the information contained in your DNA
- There are some amazing examples already of what humans and others can do to affect their epigenome
