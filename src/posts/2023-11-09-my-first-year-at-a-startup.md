My life since the pandemic has been strange. I moved to the west coast, became a dad, and started working at a startup. All of these mean different things to me, but since I’m one person, they have interweaved into this section of time in my life.

I’ll remember being stressed about learning new things, then being stressed about dealing with a toddler (and how fun and cute she is!) and then I’ll remember how beautiful and crummy the weather can be in Portland. That’ll be my memory of 2020–2023.

But working at a startup is *different* than what I’d been used to. First some —

### Context

I worked at a mid-size tech company for basically 7 years (and did a 6 month stint in the middle at Walmart Labs, a giant tech arm of a huuuuge company).

I watched that company (Appian) IPO and grow to over 1000 employees.

I knew it was getting too big for me once I started seeing people give notice who I’d never met. I was also tired of fighting the same losing battles against tech debt, so I decided to look elsewhere.

A recruiter reached out to me based on my medium writings and here I am at the [startup](https://gretel.ai/about), so where should I begin… ah yes. The biggest shock I ran into was —

### You are the *Feature* Team… and the Support Team

![(No bubble wrap!) Photo by Kier in Sight Archives on Unsplash](/img/my-first-year-at-a-startup/0_rM-2m5FFJVB3KnkG.jpg)

*(No bubble wrap!) Photo by [Kier in Sight Archives](https://unsplash.com/@kierinsightarchives?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)*

Where I used to work, we had 5–10 people on a team with smaller groups working on infrastructure. These teams were fully cross functional and were “feature teams.” They could churn out features like workers in a factory, handing them onto the ops teams and support teams like a packaged product. Eventually things might come back to bite you, but with a months long release turnaround, it took a bit of time.

> Hey Matt, remember that thing you merged 4 months ago? It broke our big customer! But we’re only telling you now that you’re on a different team, and because the support case has been open for 3 weeks and we finally found you…

When there are ~50 people at your company, if there’s a feature to be worked on, chances are if you pick that feature up, you’re going to be the main person working on it.

Maybe a few other folks will work just as much on it as you or chip in, but if there are 20 engineers, and all of them are already working their own projects, you’re probably going to take that feature from cradle to grave.

I think this is probably the *best technical part* of working at a startup. I got to get in the weeds on how our APIs worked, what our models did (AI, so hot right now), how we were going to deploy something, monitor it, troubleshoot it, etc. You learn so much when you’re not just the layer on top of the iceberg, and I think everyone in tech should join a startup for this reason. You get to understand what’s below the waterline.

The downside is, if you break it, “there’s no bubble wrap” as my old boss used to say. There isn’t an army of support engineers, cloud engineers etc. there to help you; mistakes you make are going to come back to you, and pretty quickly.

Fortunately these issues are relatively quick to resolve, because, well, you know how the sausage is made, and you can make a new batch. Another interesting thing I’ve noticed being here is that we’re —

### Not Yet Victims of Our Success

When I joined my last job, we had plenty of customers. We had people running super old versions of the software. There were government clients, private clients, partners, all sorts of fancy things that let us “radiate,” “cross-sell,” and other sales buzzwords.

And those are good things, because they give you cash to pay your employees and hire new ones.

But those customers also come with needs and wants that keep you supporting software you might prefer to dump in the recycle bin. (Can’t we just `git rm -f` this?)You become a [victim of your own success](/posts/victims-of-our-own-success/), and start being weighed down by tons of tech debt.

Startups don’t have tons of high paying customers. How can they, when they didn’t exist 5 years ago? It takes time to build a product, a sales team, a vision that someone wants to pay for. This means a lot of what startups talk about are “cash” and “runway,” i.e. how long before we have to either find more money or fire everyone. Kinda scary but, it’s how a business works when it’s not profitable.

Cash is rocket fuel, and once you run out of rocket fuel, you better be in orbit or you’re going back down.

But this is a startup’s superpower. They can try things out that won’t work with the rocket fuel of VC money, and they can quickly change tracks, because they’re not beholden to all that customer money built off old software.

Agility comes not because of better processes (we don’t have a super rigorous Agile council etc.), but because we can say “that’s not important, let’s shelve it,” and no angry customers are showing up at our door.

> A retrospective? There’s no time to look back!

We can turn our rocket ship towards a different star, as long as we have the fuel for it.

The last thing, and something that I think helps me when I feel pretty lost in what I’m doing is that I joined a place that has —

### Super Smart, Self Directed People

![(Lost in the woods!) Photo by David Kovalenko on Unsplash](/img/my-first-year-at-a-startup/0_YPP1kzkTGKofzpqC.jpg)

*(Lost in the woods!) Photo by [David Kovalenko](https://unsplash.com/@davidkovalenkoo?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com?utm_source=medium&utm_medium=referral)*

Where I used to work, there was a ton of hand holding. Pair programming with new hires, a whole month of onboarding in an academy program, and a slow ramp where you got fed SPI tickets and didn’t have to get your hands dirty that quickly. It was necessary, because a few decades of features and tech debt left a pretty complicated piece of software to understand.

I’d say it’s like taking a trip to an all inclusive resort, in a gated community. Sure it’s probably enjoyable, but you’re not going to be tested, and it’s going to feel slow.

At a startup, there’s not really any hand holding that I’ve seen. In fact, you might hear more of “we want to know what you think for this” or “we need you to build this.” (Me, the guy you just hired and doesn’t know what’s going on? OK!)

It’s like your plane that was flying to the all inclusive resort crashed in the jungle, and you and the other survivors are going to build a resort from the ground up.

But fortunately for me, everyone that crashed is super capable and can do whatever they need to get done on their own. It’s nice to not have to make a ticket and hope that Team X will be merciful and grant your request. You can just ask Jeff what he thinks about this issue you found, because Jeff built the entire feature from scratch. Or heck, you just put on the infinity gauntlet and do it yourself.

> The release is blocked because Jeff broke it? I’ll merge the fix myself!

### Summary

Working at a startup has been an awesome learning experience. If you have the opportunity to work with smart people on a small team, I’d take it before all the VC money is gone! (RIP SVB)
