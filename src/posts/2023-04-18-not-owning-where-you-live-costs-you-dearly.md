### Why Are Folks Renting?

[The national average is 36%](https://www.worthinsurance.com/post/homeowners-vs-renters-statistics#:~:text=If%20you%27re%20wondering%2C%20%22,households%20currently%20rent%20their%20home%3F). One of every three people in the US.

Renting makes sense if you’re only planning on being somewhere temporarily, or can’t qualify for a mortgage (which is true for MANY folks). But what is the real downside of renting? It’s in the numbers:

### Econ 101

I you’re paying rent you can certainly afford a mortgage payment. Unless you’re in a rent controlled apartment ([which, they have their own issues](https://freakonomics.com/podcast/why-rent-control-doesnt-work/)), you’re paying MORE than the person who owns the property does for the unit you’re living in.

It’s the simple principle of economics that, if I sell you a good, you must pay me AT LEAST my cost, with usually some sort of cushion, which is either for profit or incidentals (if I’m a non-profit).

Take an example: I own a property that I decide to rent. I haven’t paid off the mortgage, so

- I have a mortgage payment of $1000 a month.
- I also have to pay insurance, which is $50.
- I also pay property taxes, let’s say another $100.
- I’m part of an HOA, effectively adding $200 a month.
- Utilities, trash fees, another $100 a month

So with this setup, we’re at $1450 a month. If I were to rent my property, and just simply pay my mortgage and cover my costs, I’d need at least $1450, with maybe some room in case the tenant breaks something. So we’re talking $1500–1800 would be the minimum I could charge to rent the place and basically break even.

That $1500 might look very high (or very low) to you depending on where you live. (Apparently the median rent price is ~$1900). In a city in the US, you’re probably not going to find something that cheap, unless it’s a small city or the unit is FAR from the city center.

### The Cost

So let’s compare the two: the homeowner and the renter.

Using that above number,

the homeowner pays $1450 a month, and let’s say the renter pays $1800.

1 year

- Renter: **$21,600**
- Homeowner: **$17,400**

10 years

- Renter: **$210,600**
- Homeowner: **$174,000**

30 years

- Renter: **$648,000**
- Homeowner: **$522,000**

So the renter would pay (at a minimum) $100,000 more over 30 years. This is assuming a lot of things stay fixed, which would not be the case.

The homeowner would see rises in their utilities, their HOA fees, their taxes (since they’re based on property values), and most likely their insurance premiums. They would probably put money into the house to repair damage or have things inspected/updated. But if they have a fixed rate mortgage, that payment would at least stay the same.

The renter would simply see their rent go up (a lot). It would in part be due to the things the homeowner sees go up, but it would also be influenced by demand for a commodity (i.e. for the rental properties), [so that the rent could shoot up much higher](https://img.money.com/2017/06/screen-shot-2017-06-23-at-11-38-52-am.png?quality=60).

The renter faces all the factors the homeowner does, but has no control over them, nor the extra factor of the rental market itself. In all likelihood the gap would be even wider than $100,000 as far as the cost over 30 years.

That’s what we sacrifice by renting right? Well…

### The Real Cost

The real loss is not in the extra $100,000 over 30 years, although that’s significant.

What the homeowner has at the end of that time is a property worth some amount of money.

What the renter has is… nothing, except that they survived 30 years, and got to live where they picked.

Let’s assume this imaginary house is worth $250,000. With a $50,000 down payment, this gets us roughly to our $1500 a month payment.

If the house is worth *the same* in 30 years, we now have a $350,000 difference between our renter and homeowner (the extra cost plus the value of the house).

But housing prices in almost all places go up over time. [Heck, even Detroit is better now than in the 70s](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/ATNHPIUS19804Q).

This appreciation is significant, [from somewhere to 4–7%](https://www.creditkarma.com/home-loans/i/average-home-value-increase-per-year#:~:text=Since%201991%2C%20the%20average%20annual,significantly%20from%20state%20to%20state.) over the last 30 years.

At 4%, you get a house of [$250,000 being worth over **$800,000**](https://goodcalculators.com/home-appreciation-calculator/).

At 7% we’re up to **$1,900,000**.

So after 30 years of doing whatever it is you’re doing, we’re talking the difference between a millionaire and not, just on the value of your house.

### The Control

Renters in many places have power, but the ultimate power lies in homeowners. They’ve got the money and the control over what happens with their place. It might take them time, but they can evict their tenants if they need to.

A million dollars is a big cost to pay as a renter, but also being able to do what you want with your own place might be a priceless thing. Redoing a room, or changing the layout of your house, either with sweat equity or a contractor, is not something a renter gets to decide.

You get to shape your destiny as a homeowner.

As a renter, your destiny is shaped for you; you can only vote with your feet.

### But I Can’t Afford that Down Payment!

That sucks, but you can *eventually*.

You just might not be able to right now, or where you live.

You might have to move somewhere that you can afford a place, or get into a much smaller place, and save up for some time. But you can sell and trade up to a bigger and/or better place, keeping the snowball rolling of your accumulated investment.

Otherwise you’re just paying someone’s mortgage, or letting a corporation rake in your money.

Ideally you save up and help your kids out, so they can get into a place without killing themselves or paying someone’s rent for a long time. What better way to set your kids up to do well than to help them get out of the renting (losing) game.

For homeownership: own or lose millions renting, the choice is yours.
