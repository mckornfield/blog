If you want to make a difference in people’s lives, be effective

Imagine you’re given a problem to solve. Any problem.

Let’s make it very concrete, you’re selling someone a car. The problem is, the person doesn’t know what car to buy.

There are two extreme approaches:

1. Take your time to understand what the other person wants, knowing it might cost you other deals
2. Try and push the person as quickly as possible to make a purchase decision, so you have time to work with other folks

What we’re weighing here is **effectiveness (1)** vs. **efficiency (2).** They both have their tradeoffs, but with people effectiveness wins most of the time.

In his book *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People,* Stephen Covey writes about the interaction with his son, where he explains how how to maintain the lawn.

He hammers home the point that [effectiveness beats efficiency](https://www.campusrenewal.org/blog/2014/11/15/efficiency-vs-effectiveness/), because being efficient with people usually leads to *ineffective outcomes*.

### The Yin and Yang of Effectiveness and Efficiency

We can think of these two forces and Yin (female energy, moonlight) and Yang (male energy, sunlight).

A woman (or mother) generally speaking wants to engage with someone to figure out how they tick, why they think a certain way. They grow closer through getting to understand somebody and sharing and relating.

A man (or dad) generally speaking wants to solve problems quickly. They’re very busy and are trying to get things finished/ boxes checked so they can move on to the next thing.

These stereotypes mirror effectiveness and efficiency pretty well.

You want your dad to help you open the pickle jar, or move some heavy furniture, but you want your mom to console you when kids are mean to you at school. Mom cares about being effective with you, while Dad is generally more efficient but less effective.

But efficiency can really suck. You quickly solve a problem, but because you weren’t effective you:

1. Didn’t figure out what the problem actually was
2. Didn’t connect emotionally with the person
3. Gave them the wrong solution to their actual problem

### My Recent Adventure

I took the trolley in San Diego recently and got another lesson in effectiveness over efficiency.

A man got put on the trolley, and someone told him “you need to get off at the next stop and take the 995 bus.” I thought… that’s weird, is this guy lost or something?

It turns out he was lost in more way than one. He had his phone out but he could barely speak English. He said “aeroport”, so I tried a bit of Spanish that I knew, but he just furrowed his brow and started speaking into Google Translate in Russian. (I know just enough Russian to know that’s what it was, but beyond that Google Translate became our main way of talking).

He explained that he was trying to get to Los Angeles. I said that the bus that he needed to take to the airport was at the current station, but it was too late to get off so he’d have to wait until the next stop.

Then I asked him to show me his ticket. And I got more confused, because it was an Amtrak ticket. He was trying to get to the train station!

But the train had already left, so I tried to explain that to him, but realized this wasn’t going to be a “get off here and turn right” sort of deal.

So I got off the trolley and told him to get off with me. We waited and I learned a bit more about him (like his name, and that he was from Kazakstan, and had been in the US 4 days).

We took the trolley back the other way to the train station. I talked to the woman at the front desk to get him put on the next bit of public transit to LA, a bus leaving in an hour.

I walked around the building, showed him where the bus stop was, and gave him my number in case he had an issue. He was super grateful, and it only took around 30 minutes of my time, but I think I might have made his trip to LA a lot smoother. And I showed him that there are Americans that care about people who are just trying to get somewhere and have the means to (he had a card and could pay his way).

Compare that with the start of my experience, where someone just yelled at him that he had to get off at the next stop to get to the airport. They just assumed when he said he was going to LA that he was flying. But they didn’t actually know how he was trying to get there.

The main problem with being efficient is that you end up **solving the wrong problem.** If you want to make a difference in someone’s life, you have to know what the problem is first, before you can solve it.
