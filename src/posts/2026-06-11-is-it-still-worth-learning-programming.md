### Who am I to tell you if you should program?!

I learned very little programming in school. I took 3 comp-sci style classes in college, and my self directed attempts to learn were toy projects that didn’t stick with me.

But I did end up learning at least in some capacity how to program, mostly on the job and reading some textbooks while struggling at work.

[It was a stressful time with good tunes.](https://mckornfield.medium.com/paramore-virginia-running-it-consulting-d9342992f247)

So to the question “should you get a comp-sci degree?” I have no clue, since I didn’t get one and the market has changed a ton since then.

BUT I want to talk about *programming*, since I think it’s broader than an individual computer science major, and also something that has been taking a beating recently.

So should you?

Yes… and no?

Maybe?

### The Carpenter Analogy

It’s been many, many years since carpenters worked with only manual tools. The trade has a ton of mechanical tooling, in the form of basic things like [table saws](https://www.homedepot.com/b/Tools-Power-Tools-Saws-Table-Saws/N-5yc1vZ2fkokih), or more complicated things like [CnC routers](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NWDrWbCbI3s).

A carpenter has a panoply of tools they can use to cut wood, and different types of wood to cut.

You might say “why don’t carpenters just use CnC routers for everything,” but for the folks I know that do carpentry/woodworking, if they have a CnC, they have all the other stuff too.

And what was this about again? Oh right…

### What is Programming?

I’ll tell you a few things programming IS NOT

- Programming is not hand typing code from memory
- Programming is not copy pasting snippets from Stack Overflow
- Programming is not accepting tab completion
- Programming is not agentic coding

You might say “well then wtf is [programming](https://www.etymonline.com/word/program)?” I’d argue it’s:

> making a computer or computers do something you want, while preventing bad people from doing **whatever** they want

Pre-LLM/agentic coding, that meant that even with an IDE and the internet at your disposal, your goal was to create something useful that worked in the real world.

### “Your Job is Done For”

Maybe you’ve seen this meme.

![One of many sources](/img/is-it-still-worth-learning-programming/0_EYBi2GamChlxixS9.jpg)

*One of many [sources](https://www.reddit.com/r/ProgrammerHumor/comments/135iokd/ai_is_taking_over/)*

If your job is just to tell an LLM to make a website, then yeah your job is done.

Recently a family member texted me an index.html file and I thought “well, that’s not quite right.”

Non-technical people have no idea how the internet works. They have no idea what network protocols are, markup or markdown, DNS, certificate authorities, even a darn server, etc. etc. They don’t know how computers, or their phones work, beyond “TRANSISTORS!” Or maybe not even that.

Arguably none of that knowledge is programming *either*, but it’s pretty difficult to make useful programs that don’t take advantage of the Internet ecosystem without knowing how the internet works.

Similar to the carpenter, it’d be pretty scary if you had a table saw, CnC router and all the tools at a carpenter’s disposal, but didn’t invest any of the time and effort into learning how these tools worked.

Arguably you’d be a danger to yourself and to others. Making furniture and structures that fall apart, or hurting yourself while making things.

The same can be said for people using things like OpenClaw on their machines, then [exposing them to the mercy of the internet](https://openclaw.allegro.earth/), where anything on their machine could be turned over to an attacker.

So, if our jobs are just generating code, then yes, our jobs are done for. But if our job is understanding how the systems work and making sure we don’t hurt ourselves or others while developing them… then I think programmers will have jobs for the near future.

### “Tokenmaxxing vs. Tokenminning”

If you’re into vibe coding, then you’ve probably heard of tokenmaxxing. The idea that you spend as many tokens as possible, maybe for the sake of productivity, or because you’re using Gastown/GasCity to make some crazy thing.

I’d argue that if you want to learn, you need to do “**tokenminning**”, i.e., that you need to learn the *basics:* the syntax of a language, the tradeoffs to memory management, how to write a program that does *something useful*, all while keeping your token generation/agentic use to a minimum, and the code you produce to a maximum.

Recently I am just going through [rustlings](https://rustlings.rust-lang.org/?utm_source=substack&utm_medium=email) by reading the docs and typing into the IDE. *What a caveman!*

But I do feel like things stick when I take it slow and have the pain of reading compiler errors (rust has a much nicer compiler than I remember Java 8 had).

It’s not that AI coding tools are useless or that they don’t provide utility, it’s rather that they are, as one of my former coworkers used the term: *chainsaws*.

They are high powered tools that are very good at moving through work fast, but not very good (at least in my experience) at letting you understand and [slow down](https://mariozechner.at/posts/2026-03-25-thoughts-on-slowing-the-fuck-down/).

A programmer exists not just to make the computer do something useful (and stop bad guys from taking advantage of them), but also to become useful *themselves by acquiring knowledge.* To be able to build and debug upon an existing project, or build a new one, in a way that is safe, performant etc.

So should you become a programmer?

### Fewer Free Lunches

I think the time for “hey I’m a programmer! Hand me a big paycheck out of school” s probably already at its end. Here’s a graph supporting my viewpoint.

![](/img/is-it-still-worth-learning-programming/1_-NKsdSV7x3-BGgY_SQC9qQ.png.png)

That said: if you like computers, and what to make them do things, for sure learn programming. [And some AI fundamentals](https://www.elementsofai.com/) would probably be good now as well. Maybe become an AI researcher/applied scientist?

So the answer is: yes it’s still worth learning if you’re good at it or interested, **no** it’s not worth it if you think you’ll just be handed a big paycheck. The field is getting much more narrow, as companies try to replace their workers with AI powered engineers. Maybe that trend will continue, or maybe not. I suspect it will continue and become more competitive, but that doesn’t mean programmers are all going to be filing for unemployment next year.

As far as practical tips on becoming a programmer in this day and age: you’re going to want to both want to figure out how to get agentic coding to do what you want, and learn *why* you’d pick certain solutions vs. others (vs. just sending someone a link to a local HTML file!)
