For those of you without children, or who haven’t seen *Sing* or *Sing 2,* my two sentence summary:

— a small-town group of musical animals put on a once in a lifetime show in Redshore City (Las Vegas), through a series of stretched truths and stretched abilities, all while singing Top 40 songs. It has an all-star cast, who can sing fairly well (❤ Taron Edgerton) as well as some music legends (Halsey, Bono).

So why have I watched it every day for 45 days? Well, my 1 1/2 year old can’t get enough of it (granted I know, no screen time for kids, but we go out and play too 😅). She’s glued to the screen and even dances around for certain songs. Here’s what I learned from the film and from watching her.

Some spoilers ahead, so if you haven’t watched it, be warned!

### Kids Love Repetition, with Good Reason

The film centers around performers who have 3 weeks to put on a world class sci-fi musical. Plenty of scenes involve repetition and rehearsal. The same dance moves, the same songs, the same lines. Repetition is the boring but necessary step the characters take to work out the kinks in their performances, as well as grow personally when they hit stumbling blocks.

For kids watching the film, the same characters and same songs are familiar. In a dynamic world, the same scenes, same words, and same songs create stability for a kid. Though it’s repeated, music taps into our [emotions](https://creativeeccentric.wordpress.com/2012/05/17/book-commentaryreview-music-the-brain-and-ecstacy-by-robert-jourdain/#:~:text=Music%20often%20expresses%20the%20unexpressible%2C%20emotions%2C%20but%20even%20beyond%20emotions.), the language children understand before they can speak.

When I see my little one dancing around, it makes me giggle but also shows me that she’s learning from all this repetition. The characters in the film dance, and she’s dancing with them. I didn’t show her how to dance, but now she’ll grab my hand and my wife’s hand and get us to dance along with her. Repetition is how kids learn.

### Lying Will Get You in Trouble, but…

***sometimes you have to say you have something figured out before you do***

The main character, Buster Moon, is a koala show-runner that fakes his way through much of the film, saying he can get a famous singer (which he has no contact with to start), or that he was part of the audition pool for a new show (which he was most certainly not).

His truth stretching is a key lesson for all people who want to be successful or dream big. Steve Jobs called it his “[reality distortion field](https://jhargrave.medium.com/how-steve-jobs-created-the-reality-distortion-field-and-you-can-too-4ba87781adba#:~:text=Steve%20Jobs%27s%20%E2%80%9Creality%20distortion%20field,too%2C%20could%20achieve%20the%20impossible.),” and many other people, real and imaginary, have conquered the impossible by saying they can do something, or know how to do something, before they have everything figured out.

It’s a great lesson to kids both ways. Lying gets you in trouble (so tell the truth!) but also the greatest achievers in this world were impostors at some point. They said things that weren’t exactly true, stretching their abilities or the abilities of their peers so that they weren’t made out to be a liar in the end.

And Moon’s character puts on a great show after all, with the star that he promised. He stretches himself and others around him. In a key moment, his life is saved by another character (Rosita) who’s afraid of heights, but knows that if she doesn’t a daring leap to save Moon, then no one will. The fact that Moon’s life is in danger in the first place though is a consequence of his initial “truth stretching.”

### Sometimes Your Mentor is Not Your Teacher

The gorilla pianist and singer of the group, Johnny (yay [Taron Edgerton](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm5473782/)) is thrown into the deep end when he stars in a “daring battle scene” that ends up being a big dance number.

His choreographer and teacher is Klaus Kickenklober, a proboscis monkey with an accent and attitude, who makes it his personal mission to push Johnny and make an example of him.

For kids, this lesson is a harsh one: your teacher is not always your friend, and many times can be an antagonist in your life. Plenty of grade school and college/university teachers or professors can be stern or uncompromising with their students, and that can be hard for many kids or young adults to process.

Fortunately the world is a big place, and even if you don’t have the freedom to switch teachers/professors/bosses, you can switch your mentor. Johnny finds a street performing lynx named Nooshy who shows him how to really dance, coming at him from a more relaxed but still high energy/ high effort perspective.

Johnny really turns around his dancing because of Nooshy, mastering the choreography and even mastering Klaus when his choreographer tries to show him up during the final performance.

The lesson here for kids is pretty clear: you have to seek out your mentor and find someone who works with you. The world won’t deliver a mentor to you on a silver platter, and sometimes the person you do get handed will make your learning experience harder, not easier.

### Summary

- Repetition is how kids learn best
- Fake it ‘till you make it, but beware the consequences of lying
- Do your best to find a mentor that helps you, even if it’s not your teacher
