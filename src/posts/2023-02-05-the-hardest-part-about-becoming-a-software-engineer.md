### It’s Not the Algorithms

While many algorithms can be difficult to understand at first, most folks that have basic math skills can pick these things up. Some of the more advanced algorithms are beyond many folks (myself included), but fortunately you don’t use Djikstra’s algorithm very much if you aren’t building some low level solution to a path problem.

Algorithms are important but not urgent to your success as a programmer. And they are certainly no more complicated than something like postmodernism (though maybe more coherent?) or macroeconomic theory. Algorithms are just the set of steps to solve specific types of logic problems.

### It’s Not the Data Structures

Dictionaries. Sets. Lists. Tuples. Priority Queues. These are fancy terms but all of them have real world analogies that are pretty easy to reason about (I mean… dictionary kind of explains itself).

While some of the more machine specific data structures can be funky (Linked Lists and Arrays), I wouldn’t consider these things to be the barrier to entry to the industry, just like with algorithms.

### It’s Not Being In Front of a Computer

All jobs these days that pay anything decent involve being in front of a computer, a phone, or at the end of some electronic device.

Welcome to the world that Bill Gates and Steve Jobs built.

### It’s Not The “Coding”

Agonizing over what to name things, ruminating over functional vs. procedural styles. These are not the difficult parts of being a software engineer, though they are indeed frustrating.

But these types of coding decisions can always be rehashed, and with version control it’s trivial to swap between versions.

Coding is like any skill, it starts with painful practice (copy pasting, or typing someone else’s answers by hand), then maybe with some assisted practice (you write a few things but have to look a good amount up) and eventually you can write a simple program to solve a problem with very little help.

If you ever have to do anything new/complicated, you’ll still spend a lot of time visiting a lot of documentation/ looking at sample code. I’d say coding is secondary to what Software Engineers do, not their primary task. Coding is the means, the end is solving technical problems.

So what makes it hard to be a software engineer if not the algorithms, data structures, computer sitting, and coding?

### It’s The Pain

In school it’s the pain of not knowing, of being flummoxed by questions in your computer science classes. The pain of missing the logic on how to solve a problem so that you just feel small and stupid.

In coding projects it’s the pain of debugging why a build won’t work, or why your code stopped working “all of a sudden.” And it can take hours to resolve, just to get *something* into a working state. It’s the analysis paralysis that stops you from even starting a personal project.

In work, it’s failed deployments, bad releases, late nights scrambling to make a cutoff. Debugging obscure errors, dealing with old code, or bad code, or both. Dealing with a holier-than-thou developer who doesn’t know anything outside of their own little fiefdom.

But this pain is part of any profession, or any skill that you want to learn. You just have to layer on a bunch of confusing logic

If you want to enter this industry because you want a big paycheck and a kush job, you’ve come to the wrong place. People get paid well in this industry because they’re willing to tolerate the pain of appearing and *feeling* very stupid, while also firing on all cylinders to solve problems that few others would bear the pain to solve.

What you have to contend with beyond what I mentioned above are complex evolving systems that attempt to solve complex, evolving problems. All while people of varying levels of quality change the problem-space and solution-space out from under you.

You can think of Software Engineers as plumbers who have some idea of where the water is flowing, but sometimes no water flows, sometimes it flows a different way, and some days the water is tar.

Software Engineers build a very complicated plumbing network to solve for all of these situations, but there will always be *something* new. And with that something new comes new ignorance and new pain.

Other people are also actively changing how the plumbing works; sometimes they communicate it to you well and other times you find out because your room is now underwater.

My journey in this space has been a lot of failure and pain, but what gets me through it is that I *can* improve and get better, while hopefully saving people a bit of pain with a history lesson. But if you work in this space you’ll have to contend with the pain of ignorance and doubt constantly.

If you enjoy solving these types of problems and can deal with this kind of pain, Software Engineering might be for you.
