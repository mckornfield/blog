### ThePrimeagen and Lex Fridman

[Lex Fridman interviewed Michael Paulson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tNZnLkRBYA8) a few months ago, a man also known as *ThePrimeagen*. A great 5 hours listen in my view.

AI was a central part of the discussion, and ThePrimeagen is notably a skeptic on AI coming for all programming jobs.

His description of his life and work had a core to them, which is that *Michael worked hard.*

He didn’t take shortcuts, he didn’t “hack” things together and move on. He spent many, many, many hours programming (doing things like refusing to drive his wife to class because he was too busy programming).

Michael spent years streaming his programming and related discussions while he worked for Netflix, before quitting to pursue it full time.

His streaming time was spent working on his programming ability and communication ability, to the point where he can live off of his earnings just from streaming.

### Skillful Things Done Quickly Require Years of Hard Work

There’s a [famous anecdote](https://jgcontemporary.art/blogs/news/picassos-napkin-story?srsltid=AfmBOopoLahVBLhjlf_WWrCPUCj_D0m7anXTYP2KJVNEdewxJKbZIiyy) about Pablo Picasso drawing on a napkin for 5 minutes, then going to throw it away. A woman watching him asked if she could have it, to which Picasso (who was sort of a dick) asked the woman for a ton of money.

She asked *why, if it only took you 5 minutes?* And his response: *it took me40 years to draw it in 5 minutes*.

There’s the [(somewhat disputed) 10,000 hours rule](https://www.6seconds.org/2022/06/20/10000-hour-rule/), made popular by Malcolm Gladwell in *Outliers*, which echoes this point.

It might not be exactly 10,000 hours (or more importantly, *just* the 10,000 hours), but the opposite is not the case either: if you don’t put any time into something, you will *not* be the best. No one will ask for your napkin scratches.

I wrestled for one season in high school (it was rough), and I learned some pretty valuable advice from the head coach.

A high school wrestling match is 3 x 2:00 minute periods.

(Tiny brain math: 6 minutes of clock time, given no overtime).

There’s some small downtime to reset if necessary, and the match can end *earlier* if someone is pinned/tech-ed (i.e., beaten by points). But my coach told us all:

> it’s not the 6:00 minutes on the mat, but the hours and hours you put in of **good** **practice** that makes the f — king difference. If the guy you’re going against is already better than you, you have to beat him **off the mat** by practicing harder, before you beat him **on the mat**.

Our accomplishments are always finite, as is our practice time, but good, effortful practice has no replacement.

The 6 minutes in the ring or 5 minutes drawing on a napkin are only worth something if they’re backed up by minutes, hours, days, months, years of *working hard.*

### Anything Worth Anything Comes from Hard Work

Think of someone you consider a great accomplishment. Maybe a professional athlete, a super rich person, or an accomplished academic or artist.

- They (most likely) didn’t take shortcuts to get what they achieved.
- They might have had a lot more opportunity than other people

But to imagine they just sat there as achievements floated their way on a lazy river is to be blind to how much work it takes to achieve *anything valuable.*

Often what makes the work hard is that

1. It doesn’t end (incessant)
2. It doesn’t feel good (unrewarding)
3. You aren’t good to begin with (growth)

For a personal example, I trained for and ran a marathon this year.

I don’t know what I expected, but I ran a half to a full marathon’s worth of miles *every week* in the month before the race before tapering down. These weren’t adrenaline fueled, hell yeah I’m going to finish the race, sort of runs. They were “let’s run around the track for 3 hours until my headphones die,” sort of runs, or “hey my toenail fell off.”

![Race was in May, so you can see the taper. ~8.5 in March, 8.1 miles in April, 8 a day is 56 miles a week, about half of which I ran (~28 ish miles)](/img/work-harder-not-smarter/1__Vvtq9DiBXB5s_gfloaUKA.jpeg.jpg)

*Race was in May, so you can see the taper. ~8.5 in March, 8.1 miles in April, 8 a day is 56 miles a week, about half of which I ran (~28 ish miles)*

But was it worth it in the end? Did the world change after running 26.2 miles?

Yes. I had proven to myself that I could do something pretty hard and walk away (granted, it was a very painful walking away).

And the 4 hours it took me to run that marathon were built of days, weeks, months of running, running, running.

Everything in my life (and probably in yours) that has been some notable achievement (getting into a decent school, getting a good job, getting married, having a kid) has been the result of a lot more work than I bargained for.

While shortcuts do exist, in the end they aren’t what real achievement is built of.

### Why Not Smarter?

“Working Smarter” I think is an illusion. If that means using a tool, method or trick to slim down the amount of time you need to do something, you’re most likely just shuffling around the time spent on it.

If you speed through something (“work smarter”), it will turn out to be:

1. Crap/slop
2. Something you have to rewrite

If it’s crap, then the amount of time people spend reading it will be commensurate with the time you spent writing it (0), and if it’s something you have to rewrite, well then… what time have you really saved?

There are two ways to do things:

1. Correct
2. Again

Technically there’s option (3), crap, but… no one gives a crap about crap, unless they have to shovel it, so it doesn’t earn a bullet.

When you “work smarter”, the real question is, how did you get smarter? Did you download [kung fu](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6vMO3XmNXe4) to your brain? Or did you spend hours and hours getting your butt kicked, [until you finally are able to kick butt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kP0NZOidgJw&list=PLfpps68JWqSubYY0BV1iPCg5C6dp9LXLl&index=2)?

Behind every person who can “work smarter” is a kid who went to school, had a tutor, and then went to school on Saturday. Who stayed up trying to do things on their own and figure s — t out.

There is no such thing as working smarter, there is *working harder* to build habits and systems that make you *work better*. If working smarter isn’t Picasso drawing on a napkin, then it’s working *less*, which, eventually, people will find out you’re not pulling your weight.

You might also think I’m talking about “your job”, but I think this extends to anything: taking care of a kid, volunteering, your school, your work, anything that comes out of your mouth or that you do with your arms or legs.

Do the work, otherwise we’ll do it for you, and you can find work elsewhere.
