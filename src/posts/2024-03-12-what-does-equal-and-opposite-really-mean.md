### My Degree is Good for Trivia

These days I work in software, but my physics knowledge can help if there’s a trivia question about science, or (as the article points out), explaining some things you might have heard but don’t make all that much sense to you.

Hopefully this helps if you’re learning physics or if you hear some physics concepts and think “that can’t be right…”

What I’ll seek to clarify is the misconceptions around equal and opposite reactions (Newton’s third law).

### A Reaction, Well, what’s the Action?

Before we get to reactions, it’s good to start with *actions*. Things that cause you to speed up or slow down are the “actions” in this parlance, or “forces” to use the right physics terms.

If you’re not moving or moving at a constant speed, there’s no “net force” on you. You just stay still or in motion. (Newton’s 1st Law)

If all of a sudden you start speeding up (“hit the gas!”) then you’re experiencing a net force. (Newton’s Second Law). As a person, you can feel the chair press into you of a vehicle as you speed up or fly down a hill on your bike.

That’s the “action.” Now, to explain the most confusing law, we’ll use a tool from physics, our handy-dandy —

### Force Diagrams

Before we go a bit further, it’s useful to explain what a force diagram is. I’m going to leave the math behind (since that can scare people) and just focus on the *forces*, since this alone causes confusion around the “equal and opposite reaction.”

Here is one of the first force diagrams you’ll see in a physics setting, a block on a ramp with *no friction*. Why no friction? Well because friction makes things confusing (there are different kinds!), so we ignore it early on.

There’ one force going down (Gravity!) and there’s another force going up from the ramp, because physical things don’t phase through each other (electrons repel!). This other force is called “the Normal Force”, because “normal” in this sense means “perpendicular” or “orthogonal”, fancy words for “90 degrees from”.

So there, you have two forces, and the “net force”, or the force that results when you add them, is a force that causes the block to slide down the ramp.

### The Nail and the Hammer

Imagine you’re hammering a nail into a wall. You pull the hammer back, line it up with the nail head that you’re gingerly grasping, and WHAM, strike it true. The nail buries itself in the wall, and you hammer it a few more times to make it flush.

“Every actions has an EQUAL and OPPOSITE reaction” might confuse you then. You might say to yourself “well how did the hammer push the nail into the wall?”

Let’s get our force diagrams out. The hammer is hitting the nail and applies a force. The hammer is the big block and the nail is the small block.

Great! So where do we draw this “equal and opposite” reaction. Your intuition might produce something like this:

But if these forces are “equal and opposite”, then the nail doesn’t move.

Gee Mister, how does Newton’s third law doesn’t make any sense?

Well, hopefully the force diagram above sticks out to you as WRONG. Clearly the nail doesn’t get hit with the hammer and present some sort of *immovable object*. What happens is the equal and opposite force is applied *on the hammer*, like so.

![Ah, much better](/img/what-does-equal-and-opposite-really-mean/1_PNh7fX08iInL3o-Id9UCGQ.jpeg.jpg)

*Ah, much better*

The key fact of the “equal and opposite reaction” is that these reaction forces are *never* on the same object. They’re usually on whatever is doing the pushing/pulling.

And this makes sense. When you swing the hammer, it gets stopped (both by the nail and by the wall pushing on the nail, which pushes back to the hammer). But the hammer is much bigger than the nail, so this equal and opposite force has a different effect on how much the hammer moves. The hammer comes to a stop, and the nail buries itself into the wall.

Part of Newton’s second law is big things don’t change speed as much. Slamming the breaks on a train takes a lot more time/effort than the breaks on a bicycle.

### Summary

So remember: the forces are on different objects! That way, things can move, and our nails don’t just fall to the ground when we hammer them.

Lastly: when Chuck Norris “pushes the Earth down”, technically that is *true*, but the change in the Earth’s momentum is not measurable by any tool that we possess (except maybe Chuck Norris’ eyes).
