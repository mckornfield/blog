### What do I mean by Leverage?

The first few definitions of “leverage” refer to financial terms. I’m referring to the definition:

> (to) use (something) to maximum advantage.

If you think of a lever, or any object that pivots around a point, there’s basically two points you can push on that object to demonstrate “high” and “low” leverage; near the center for low, and at the edge for high.

A good example of a lever you have just in reach is your own arm. Hold your arm out in front of you, with the elbow pointed down and your fingers pointed up.

Now, with the same amount of force:

- Push on your palm in either direction with your other hand
- Push right near your elbow in either direction with your other hand

Notice that your arm doesn’t move much when you push near the elbow but it moves a good deal when you push on your hand. You can push on the edge of an open door or near the hinge and see the same thing.

For the same effort (force)

- High leverage -> Big change
- Low leverage -> Small change

This is true for all aspects of your own life. There are always activities you can work on that are high leverage or low leverage, and your time is finite, so the best thing you can do for yourself is to choose as many high leverage activities over low leverage ones that you can.

### High vs. Low Leverage Activities in Physical Health

The way that you stay fit/ lose weight is by *breathing the weight out.* Your body breaks down extra calories when you exercise, your heart rate increases, and your breathing rate increases as you add in more oxygen to your system and *breathe out more CO2* (i.e. push out more calories). You also radiate some of that weight as heat.

Once you have a set of muscles, you [burn more calories sitting](https://www.argusleader.com/story/news/brandon/2019/03/01/fitness-do-more-muscles-mean-more-calories-burned/3027737002/#:~:text=Resting%20muscle%20tissue%20burns%20about,burn%20THAT%20many%20more%20calories.), but you’ll still have to maintain that muscle, otherwise it’s very quickly going to turn over to fat.

So where am I going with this strange biology lesson?

Well, think about low leverage activities you could do that don’t really move the needle on your physical health. These include:

- Counting calories
- Counting your steps (10k step club!)
- Switching from regular sweeteners to artificial sweeteners
- Any sort of fad diet

You’ll notice what all these have in common is meticulously avoiding doing the hard thing: *sweating and breathing hard.* What you eat is for sure important, but you can’t get around the fact that your body is meant to be worked; you sweat for a reason.

The fittest people you know do these high leverage things:

- Intense cardiovascular exercise 2–4 times a week
- Intense anaerobic (weight based) exercise 2–4 times a week
- Some form of less intense exercise every day (stretching, yoga, walking)
- Fried/ fatty/ unhealthy foods once a week *max*
- A form of contest/race to train for at a 3–6 months cadence OR a sport you play on a regular cadence

Don’t bother counting or trying fad things. [Join a gym or get a workout app](/posts/running-your-first-spartan-race/). Play a sport. But get sweating, and don’t eat crap.

### High and Low Leverage Activities in Software

The software industry is getting [laid off in droves](https://tech.co/news/tech-companies-layoffs) these past few months. Why? There’s more than one reason, but it’s a value assessment on the part of these large companies. It comes down to

- I have 100s/1000s/10,000s of employees that I pay 100k-500k AND
- A lot of them are engaged in activities that employers don’t consider necessary

There’s a lot of stack ranking and laying off going on, but what makes a low performer at a company?

It can be someone that’s lazy, sure, but from my perspective it can be summarized as “someone who engages in a lot of low leverage activities.”

What’s a low leverage activity in software? There’s a lot, but a short and very opinionated list might be:

- Refactoring for the sake of code cleanliness (instead of maintainability)
- Holding folks hostage in code reviews to make a ticket drag longer
- Arguing for points that you don’t necessarily hold, but you feel represent “a view that someone might hold”
- Doing tickets that *do* result in code changes but *don’t* result in any significant change for an end-user
- Working on a system that never comes to fruition

I’d hazard most of the people that were laid off worked on things that either no one cared about or didn’t go anywhere.

Lots of software changes in the world are “vaporware,” i.e. they result in incomplete systems or deliverables that never materialize, or worse they are “shelfware”, i.e. software that is put on a shelf and never used.

What could be a set of high leverage activities to counteract the above? Well…

- Only refactor if it makes the code more *maintainable,* and do it *as you make useful changes.*
- Don’t block people in Code Reviews. Make follow up tickets or sit with someone to get their ticket over the finish line faster.
- Only argue against something if you believe in it. Otherwise save debate for when it’s necessary.
- Don’t work on tickets that don’t provide much value. Close as *won’t do*.
- Try as early as possible to get a system into a usable state (i.e. lead with value, backfill with polish)

Some companies make it hard to accomplish the more high leverage activities I’ve described…

So quit! Or do whatever you can to buck the system!

Find a company that lets you do high leverage things. You will be better off and so will the company.

### In Summary

Remember, your arm moves a lot more when you push on your palm with the same amount of force vs. when you push near your elbow.

Find the high leverage activities in your life. For the amount of time you put in, you’ll get a lot farther.
