### A Coworker of Mine Once Said…

That a career is not really like a ladder. The term “corporate ladder” is a bit of a misnomer. She told me “work is like a jungle gym.”

Huh.

She explained that to go up, you have to go *over* first. Sometimes the way up is much slower, more difficult, and ultimately less good for you as a person, than going over. Going over can mean a new job within a company, or a new job *outside* that company, but over means exactly that; you didn’t stay in your role and move into the senior version of yourself at the same company.

Let’s go over some concrete examples that I went through in my career.

### Associate Consultant -> Product Engineer -> Software Engineer

This was my first set of movements “over” at my first company. It was a great company for a lot of reasons (good people, decent pay), but the role I was in (an Associate Consultant making just over $70K) wasn’t one that I ultimately loved.

The technical aspects and technical problems were interesting to me, but the people and process side were *draining* for me. I consider myself at best an ambivert, but more likely an introvert that does OK in small group settings. Presenting or running larger meetings was a drain on me.

So one of the heads of the consulting department asked me if I was interested in moving into a new initiative within the engineering department, and I jumped into it with two feet.

I had become somewhat decent with using the expressions language our company used (a domain specific language), which let me wear the title of “Product Engineer”, someone in between a software engineer and a consultant, who could still work on building engineering products.

I learned as best I could the byzantine build system (which was just containerizing at the time), and struggled my way through learning Java (in retrospect, the 10+ year old Java codebase was more of the struggle).

It was a time of real transition for me (I also moved in my future wife, but at the time girlfriend, so I was really pulling out all the stops).

With enough time inside work and outside of work learning what it meant to write software, I earned the title of Software Engineer II after 2–3 years, and was making over $100,000.

In some sense I was undeserving of the title; other folks had been developing software for much longer than I had (either via CS degrees or just from personal experience).

On the other hand, it had taken me longer than many of my now peers to reach SE II, but I had a lot more domain specific knowledge than they did. And moving around on the jungle gym meant I took a longer path. I had to also scramble a lot more, as I was in unfamiliar environments (IDEs, Terminals, Git, Jenkins) that I had to make my own.

SE II at my job was a standard first year promotion for an entry level Software Engineer, and it had taken me over 2 years to reach it. But I was once again ready to move around the jungle gym.

### Software Engineer II -> Software Engineer III -> Senior Software Engineer

At the time I made a move that I considered a huge mistake, but in retrospect I think was a great learning opportunity.

I decided to leave my job, where I liked most of my peers, but was dissatisfied with the work and technology. So I took a job at Walmart Labs (didn’t interview well elsewhere, like at Google). Walmart Labs is Walmart’s tech arm (think AWS but lower TC). I immediately found two things:

1. The tech stack was way better AND could be worse (mainframes!)
2. The quality of the people really ran the gambit

There were a couple folks I worked with that were solid, but the tech lead was impossible for me to work with.

Within 3 months of me joining, 2 others on the team had quit, and I had a strong feeling it was because of our tech lead.

After 6 months of learning a ton, but ultimately feeling squished under this person’s thumb, I came back to my first company as a Senior Software Engineer.

My total compensation bumped up to 135k, but I’m sure to some of the more faithful folks I’d worked with, I was seen as “not loyal” or “opportunistic.” I’ll address this in a follow up article.

### Senior Software Engineer -> Junior Manager

This move was probably the weirdest. I liked the idea of managing folks, so I took on the role, but it was ultimately a terrible time to become a manager (this was right during COVID). I had a bunch of virtual meetings with folks, but I have a pretty strong opinion when it comes to virtual management: it doesn’t really work.

My boss at the time told me about “managing by walking around”, which I strongly believe in. This was just stopping by people’s desks (hopefully not blowing people’s productivity out the window) and interacting with people organically to talk about what they were doing.

Check-in meetings/ scheduled 1on1s are necessary evils in a completely virtual setup, but they really don’t invite the types of conversation that I think are as engaging as something like seeing what someone is up to *in the moment*.

On the other hand, I took a company provided MBA program and read a bunch of soft skills books that I think helped me not just be a better manager, but a better employee. I think the decision to try the management track was the right one, but timing it with COVID was not.

### Junior Manager -> Senior Site Reliability Engineer

Along the way I’d gained random bits of experience in performance testing, monitoring and developer experience. It was enough to make a play for a role at a startup as an SRE, which is where I’m at now and which I’ve already [written about](https://betterprogramming.pub/my-first-year-at-a-startup-0b112bf66890).

I’ll say total compensation wise, startups are probably lower (unless the options become something real, it’s essentially monopoly money, compared with real valued stock options/RSUs I was paid elsewhere).

But you have so much more of an opportunity to learn and wear many hats at a startup.

In my time at a startup I’ve:

- Helped with customer deployments
- Dealt with private networking security
- Done my best to support the build system
- Worked with GCP and Azure (two clouds that I’d largely avoided at my former employer that was all in on AWS)

I also moved to a fully remote company and abandoned my management mantle, which I think was ultimately good (maybe I’m not meant to be a manager at all, but I’m *definitely* not meant to be a virtual manager). Fully remote also beats being one of a few remote employees (since our company was big into the return to office push).

This move sideways also allowed me to invest more time in my writing and get a much stronger grasp of what it means to do “[ML Engineering.](https://levelup.gitconnected.com/the-latest-reinvention-of-the-system-admin-mlops-d73abdbba29b)”

That’s my journey; in another article, I’ll go over the takeaways from moving around the jungle gym, but for me, I learned much more by climbing around than I did by climbing up.
